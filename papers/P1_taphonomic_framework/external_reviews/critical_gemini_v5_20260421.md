**Referee Report for "The Volcanic Detection Horizon in Java: An Archaeological Puzzle and a Research Program"**

**1. SUMMARY**
This manuscript proposes that the apparent scarcity of pre-10th century archaeological sites in volcanic Java, particularly open-air settlements, is primarily a consequence of cumulative volcanic burial rather than a reflection of past population densities or cultural practices. The authors, computational researchers, derive preliminary site-level sedimentation rates (2.4â6.2 mm/yr) from four Hindu-Buddhist monumental sites (Dwarapala Singosari, Sambisari, Kedulan, Kimpulan) and project these rates to suggest that pre-Hindu surfaces now lie several meters to 10 meters deep, beyond the reach of conventional surface survey. The paperâs central contribution is framed as a research program outlining specific geoarchaeological studies (OSL dating, tephrochronology, targeted coring) necessary to rigorously test this "detection horizon" hypothesis.

**2. OVERALL ASSESSMENT**
Reject. The manuscript presents an interesting and important problem, but its empirical foundation is too weak and its claims too speculative for publication in a Q1 journal like *Journal of Anthropological Archaeology* without substantial new data. It reads more as a research proposal or a problem statement than a rigorous empirical contribution.

**3. MAJOR CONCERNS**

1.  **Overreach of Claims vs. Empirical Support:** The paper frequently hints at an "invisible civilization" or suggests that the "apparent chronological primacy of Kutai may therefore reflect differential preservation rather than temporal priority" (Sect. 5.3, Conclusion). While the authors explicitly state in Section 5.5 that they "make no claim about site-type composition... population size, cultural complexity, or the broader interpretive question of what a buried pre-Hindu Javanese record might contain," the framing throughout the abstract, introduction, and discussion consistently implies a significant, buried pre-Hindu presence. This speculative framing, particularly in a Q1 archaeological journal, is not supported by the four preliminary, monument-derived calibration points. The paper's "challenger pivot" (as noted in the LaTeX comments) is evident in this tension between stated limitations and implied significance. *Why it matters:* Such claims, if not rigorously substantiated, can mislead future research and funding priorities, diverting attention from other plausible explanations for archaeological absence (e.g., actual low population density, non-durable material culture, different settlement patterns). *How to address:* The authors must either significantly temper the interpretive language to strictly match the presented data (i.e., focus solely on the *geomorphological* detection horizon, not its *anthropological* implications for "civilization") or provide the empirical data from the proposed research program to support such claims.

2.  **Reliance on a Qualitative Colonial Observation as Primary Anchor:** The entire Kelud-system calibration, and thus a significant portion of the "Java-wide" consistency argument, hinges on the 1803 Engelhard observation of the Dwarapala statues being "half buried" (Sect. 3.2, 4.1, 5.6). This is a qualitative, anecdotal colonial description, not a precise geoarchaeological measurement. The authors acknowledge this as a limitation (Sect. 5.6) and propose archival verification as part of their research program (Sect. 5.7). However, presenting a primary calibration point based on such uncertain data in a Q1 journal is unacceptable. *Why it matters:* If this foundational rate is inaccurate, the entire projection model for the Kelud system, and by extension the "order-of-magnitude agreement across two volcanic systems," is compromised. This undermines the core empirical claim of the paper. *How to address:* This cannot be addressed without new data. The paper should be resubmitted *after* the archival verification is completed and, ideally, supplemented with new, direct geoarchaeological measurements from the Kelud basin.

3.  **The "KutaiâLiangan Natural Experiment" and Confounders:** The paper frames the Kutai (Kalimantan, 400 CE, surface finds, no volcanoes) and Liangan (Java, 590 CE, deep burial, preserved wood) contrast as a "natural experiment" (Sect. 5.3). While the contrast in burial regime is evident, attributing the archaeological differences *primarily* to taphonomy ("The contrast is not primarily a contrast in past occupation or institutional complexity---it is a contrast in burial regime") is an oversimplification. Kutai is known from monumental stone inscriptions, a specific cultural practice reflecting a particular political and religious context. Pre-Hindu Java may have had different material culture, settlement patterns, or political organizations that did not prioritize durable stone monuments or inscriptions. The archaeological record of Kalimantan itself is not exhaustively known for pre-400 CE periods. *Why it matters:* This framing risks reducing complex historical and cultural processes to a single geomorphological variable, which is anthropologically problematic. It assumes a baseline of comparable cultural practices and monumental investment that is not demonstrated. *How to address:* The authors should acknowledge the multiple, interacting factors that contribute to archaeological visibility, including cultural choices regarding material culture, monumentality, and settlement location, rather than presenting the contrast as a simple "natural experiment" primarily driven by taphonomy. The paper should focus on the *potential* for burial, not the *certainty* of a buried civilization.

4.  **Generalizability of Monument-Derived Rates to Open Settlements:** All four primary calibration points are stone monuments (\textit{candi}) (Sect. 5.6). Monuments are not ideal proxies for landscape-scale aggradation in open habitation sites. Their construction (platforms, foundations) can alter local topography, trap sediment, or be subject to different maintenance regimes. While the authors acknowledge this limitation (Sect. 5.5, 5.6) and propose OSL dating and tephrochronology in non-monumental contexts (Sect. 5.7), the current paper relies entirely on monument data for its rate projections. *Why it matters:* The central hypothesis concerns the detectability of *settlements*, not just monuments. If the rates derived from monuments are significantly different from those in open habitation areas, the entire "detection horizon" for settlements is miscalculated. *How to address:* This is another concern that cannot be fully addressed without new data. The paper should be reframed as a methodological proposal for *how* to derive these rates for settlements, rather than presenting monument-derived rates as preliminary anchors for settlement burial.

5.  **Utility of the Compiled Site Dataset:** The authors compiled a dataset of 666 archaeological sites in East Java, of which 391 are geocoded (Sect. 3.1). They explicitly state that this dataset "cannot test this hypothesis directly" (Abstract) and "is not presented in this paper as a test of the framework" (Sect. 1, 5.4) due to biases. While acknowledging bias is good, the inclusion of this dataset and its detailed description (Sect. 3.1) feels like filler if it serves no analytical purpose in the paper. The claim that "Documenting that this surface record cannot distinguish low population from taphonomic erasure is itself an analytic contribution" (Sect. 1) is a weak justification for presenting a dataset that is then dismissed. *Why it matters:* In a Q1 journal, every component of the data and methods section should contribute directly to the paper's argument or its empirical testing. If the dataset cannot be used, its detailed description is superfluous. *How to address:* Either remove the detailed description of the compiled site dataset, or, more constructively, use it to illustrate the *magnitude* of the bias (e.g., by comparing the distribution of known sites to areas of high predicted burial, even if not a formal test).

**4. METHODOLOGICAL CONCERNS**

*   **Statistical Methods:** The statistical basis for the sedimentation rates is extremely limited. With $n=4$ calibration points (Sect. 3.3), the arithmetic mean of 4.4 mm/yr and its standard deviation are descriptive only, as the authors correctly note. The "wide confidence bounds" are not quantified, which is a significant omission for a computational paper. The linear projection model (Sect. 3.5) is acknowledged as a "first-order approximation," but the lack of any uncertainty propagation from the rate range (2.4-6.2 mm/yr) into the projected depths (e.g., confidence intervals around the green band in Fig. 2) makes the projections appear more precise than they are. The discussion of compaction (Sect. 4.3) is qualitative; a simple model for how compaction might reduce projected depths would strengthen the argument.
*   **Evidence Multi-channel or Hidden Dependence:** The four primary anchors, while geographically separated, share a "shared methodological basis (burial depth divided by elapsed time since dated construction)" (Sect. 1, 3.3). The 51-pair literature compilation is explicitly stated to "draws on the same corpus of colonial and volcanological reports and therefore inherits similar selection biases" (Sect. 3.4, 4.2). This means the "multiple lines of evidence" are not truly independent. They are all subject to similar biases (monumentality, accidental discovery, colonial reporting). This hidden dependence weakens the claim of consistency across different lines of evidence.
*   **Comparisons (Philippines, Japan, Thailand):** The paper does not make explicit comparisons to archaeological sites in the Philippines, Japan, or Thailand. It focuses on the Kutai (Kalimantan) contrast. As noted in Major Concern #3, the Kutai comparison is problematic due to numerous uncontrolled confounders (cultural, historical, research intensity). While the paper states it's a "testable geomorphological claim that does not depend on the full package of historical assumptions" (Sect. 1), the subsequent interpretation of the Kutai-Java contrast often relies on precisely those assumptions.
*   **Falsification Criterion:** The paper's strength lies in its proposed research program (Sect. 5.7), which *outlines* how the hypothesis could be falsified (e.g., "A negative result at a statistically adequate sample... would be material evidence against the detection-horizon interpretation"). However, the current paper does not *perform* any falsification. It presents a hypothesis and preliminary supporting data, but no direct tests. This is why it reads more as a research proposal.
*   **Computational Methods Transparency and Reproducibility:** The computational methods for data compilation (OSM, Wikidata, Wikipedia scraping) and geocoding are described, and a GitHub repository is promised. The projection model is simple arithmetic. This aspect seems transparent and reproducible for the computational steps described.

**5. SPECIFIC CLAIMS TO CHALLENGE**

*   "The contrast is not primarily a contrast in past occupation or institutional complexity---it is a contrast in burial regime." (Sect. 5.3)
    *   **Category:** (c) Overreach. This claim minimizes the role of cultural factors in the archaeological record and assumes a level of pre-Hindu Javanese complexity that is not empirically demonstrated in this paper.
*   "The apparent chronological primacy of Kutai may therefore reflect differential preservation rather than temporal priority." (Sect. 5.3)
    *   **Category:** (b) Plausible but under-supported. While differential preservation is a plausible factor, the evidence presented is insufficient to elevate this to a primary explanation over other factors (e.g., actual temporal priority, differential cultural practices).
*   "The archaeological record of volcanic Java has been sampled largely where the process has failed to proceed completely, and the apparent chronological primacy of Kutai may therefore reflect differential preservation rather than temporal priority." (Sect. 5.3)
    *   **Category:** (a) Well-supported (first part), (b) plausible but under-supported (second part). The first part is a valid critique of archaeological sampling bias in volcanic landscapes. The second part, as above, is plausible but not sufficiently supported to be a strong claim.
*   "The framework's value, we argue, is not that it demonstrates a buried civilisation. It is that it characterises a detection horizon." (Sect. 5.4, Conclusion)
    *   **Category:** (b) Plausible but under-supported. While this is the stated intent, the "detection horizon" itself is characterized by highly preliminary rates derived from a very small, biased sample. The *idea* is valuable, but its current characterization is weak.
*   "The apparent archaeological absence of pre-10th century open-air sites in volcanic Java cannot be read as evidence of cultural absence unless that horizon is first measured directly." (Sect. 5.4, Conclusion)
    *   **Category:** (a) Well-supported. This is a crucial methodological point and a significant contribution of the paper. It correctly identifies a logical fallacy in interpreting negative evidence without understanding taphonomic processes.

**6. WHAT THE PAPER DOES WELL**

1.  **Identifies a Critical Problem:** The paper highlights a fundamental and under-explored problem in Javanese archaeology: the systematic bias in archaeological visibility imposed by cumulative volcanic sedimentation. This is a genuinely important issue that needs attention.
2.  **Proposes a Clear, Testable Geomorphological Mechanism:** The concept of a "detection horizon" driven by cumulative volcanic burial, distinct from catastrophic burial, is clearly articulated and offers a specific, testable geomorphological mechanism for archaeological absence.
3.  **Outlines a Concrete, Actionable Research Program:** Section 5.7, detailing the need for primary-source archival work, OSL dating, tephrochronology, and targeted coring in non-monumental contexts, is excellent. It provides a clear roadmap for future, rigorous fieldwork that directly addresses the limitations of the current paper. This is the strongest part of the manuscript.

**7. WHAT THE PAPER SHOULD BE, IF NOT THIS**
Given its current state, the paper would be better suited as a detailed research proposal for a funding agency, a methodological framework paper for a geoarchaeology journal (e.g., *Geoarchaeology* or *Journal of Archaeological Science: Reports*), or a more modest "problem statement" paper for *Journal of Anthropological Archaeology* that explicitly defers empirical claims until the proposed fieldwork is completed. It could also be published as a "Commentary" or "Perspective" piece, emphasizing the methodological challenge and the proposed solutions, rather than as a full empirical research article.

**Summary for Editor:**
This manuscript proposes that cumulative volcanic burial explains the scarcity of pre-10th century archaeological sites in Java, projecting burial depths of several meters to 10 meters for early historical periods. While the paper identifies a critical and under-explored problem in Javanese archaeology and outlines an excellent research program for future fieldwork, its current empirical foundation is too weak for publication in *Journal of Anthropological Archaeology*. The preliminary sedimentation rates are derived from a very small sample of stone monuments, with the primary Kelud-system anchor relying on a qualitative colonial observation. The claims regarding an "invisible civilization" and the "natural experiment" of Kutai vs. Java overreach the presented data, which lacks rigorous controls for cultural and historical confounders. I recommend rejection, but strongly encourage the authors to pursue the proposed fieldwork and resubmit a revised manuscript with new, direct geoarchaeological data from non-monumental contexts.
