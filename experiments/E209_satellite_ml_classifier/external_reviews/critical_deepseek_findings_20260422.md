## Review
**Referee Report: Manuscript E209**

**1. SUMMARY**
The manuscript presents a computational archaeology study applying supervised machine learning (Random Forest and Gradient Boosting) to multi-season Sentinel-2 satellite imagery. Its central claim is that a classifier trained on spectral features can distinguish locations containing archaeological structures—including deeply buried sites—from non-archaeological locations in the volcanic landscapes of Java, with a reported mean AUC of 0.844. The proposed contribution is a novel, scalable method for archaeological prospection in a challenging tropical volcanic environment, with the ultimate goal of generating high-probability candidate locations for ground verification. The authors interpret their results as evidence of a real, generalizable signal and as support for a broader "taphonomic-erasure framework."

**2. OVERALL ASSESSMENT**
**Reject.** The manuscript, in its current form, presents a methodological proof-of-concept that is fundamentally disconnected from archaeological reality and makes claims that are unsupported by, and in some cases directly contradicted by, the presented evidence and established field knowledge.

**3. MAJOR CONCERNS**
1.  **Catastrophic Misrepresentation of the Training Data (Section 2):** The paper's foundational premise is flawed. The "soft positives" (106 known *candi*) are overwhelmingly Hindu-Buddhist monuments from the 8th-15th centuries CE. The "hard positives" (6 buried sites) are also exclusively Hindu-Buddhist structures (e.g., Sambisari, Kedulan, Badut). The classifier is therefore trained to recognize the spectral signature of *Hindu-Buddhist monumental architecture and its immediate environs*. It is **mathematically impossible** for this model to detect an "invisible," "pre-Hindu," or "archaeologically erased" civilization, as no such exemplars exist in the training set. The model can only find more of what it has already seen: sites broadly similar to known Hindu-Buddhist temples. This invalidates the paper's central interpretive framework.
2.  **Complete Lack of Archaeological and Taphonomic Grounding:** The manuscript reads as an exercise in remote sensing analytics, not an archaeological paper. There is no discussion of site formation processes in volcanic landscapes beyond a generic "buried under ash." The critical variables—ash layer thickness, compaction, mineralogy, subsequent pedogenesis, and their differential effects on spectral reflectance—are ignored. The assumption that a 10m pixel resolution Sentinel-2 signal can consistently penetrate 4-7 meters of volcanic overburden to detect subsurface structures is presented as a given, not a hypothesis requiring rigorous geophysical validation. The "taphonomic-erasure framework" is a narrative, not a testable model derived from the region's complex stratigraphy.
3.  **Methodological Circularity and Data Leakage:** The "leave-one-hard-positive-out" validation is presented as strong evidence of generalizability. However, these six "hard positives" are not independent of the "soft positives." They are the same class of feature: Hindu-Buddhist temples. Withholding one temple while training on 105 others and 5 more temples is not a test of finding something novel; it is a test of finding a temple slightly different from the other temples. The high scores are expected, not surprising. True independent validation would require withholding all *candi* from a geographically distinct region not represented in the training set.
4.  **The "Negative" Control Problem:** The "hard negatives" (5 locations) and "random negatives" (73 locations) are defined purely by the *absence* of a known archaeological point within 5km. In a landscape like Java, where systematic archaeological survey is incomplete, this is an operationally useless and logically dangerous definition. A "negative" could easily be an undiscovered site, a non-monumental settlement (e.g., a pre-Hindu habitation site, which the paper claims to seek), or a landscape modified by ancient agriculture. The classifier is likely learning to distinguish the specific land-use/vegetation patterns associated with *known temple precincts* from a poorly defined "other." This does not equate to detecting "archaeological presence" in a general sense.
5.  **Uncritical Interpretation of Model Output:** The top features (dry-season NDWI/NDVI means and ring metrics) are interpreted as a "subtle multi-feature pattern" indicative of buried archaeology. A far more parsimonious explanation, consistent with 25 years of field experience in East Java, is that these features correlate with *modern* conditions: temple sites are often maintained clearings, tourist areas, or located on specific geomorphic units (e.g., river terraces, foothills) with distinct hydrology and vegetation. The model may be detecting the modern anthropogenic footprint or natural landforms preferred for temple construction, not a spectral ghost of buried masonry.

**4. METHODOLOGICAL CONCERNS**
*   **Statistical Appropriateness:** The small sample size (N=190) and high feature count (51) create a high risk of overfitting, despite cross-validation. The reported AUC variance (0.767 to 0.945) is not "tight"; a range of 0.178 on a 0-1 scale is substantial and indicates high sensitivity to the specific data partition.
*   **Evidence Multi-Channel?** No. All 51 features are derived from the same Sentinel-2 optical data, just calculated with different indices and seasonal composites. They are highly inter-correlated, as the feature importance list suggests (multiple NDWI/NDVI variants). This is a single, diffuse optical signal, not multiple independent lines of evidence.
*   **Comparisons/Controls:** The manuscript does not engage in comparative analysis with other regions (Philippines, Japan, Thailand). The only control is the flawed negative set. There is no control for confounding variables like modern land use, soil type (beyond the generic "volcanic"), or topography.
*   **Falsification Criterion:** The question posed in Section 7 reveals a fundamental problem. The proposed framework is structured to be unfalsifiable. If GPR finds something, it confirms the method. If GPR finds nothing, it can be explained away by depth or preservation failure. A proper falsification test would be: *If the top-K predicted locations, after rigorous ground-truthing involving coring or excavation to a depth exceeding the local volcanic sedimentation model, yield a hit rate statistically indistinguishable from random chance within similar geomorphic units, then the classifier is not useful for detecting buried sites.*

**5. SPECIFIC CLAIMS TO CHALLENGE**
*   **Claim:** "Dry-season spectral contrast IS informative about archaeological presence in volcanic tropical Java." **(C) Overreach.** The evidence is only informative about the presence of surface and near-surface features *spectrally or contextually similar to known Hindu-Buddhist temples*. It says nothing about archaeological presence broadly defined.
*   **Claim:** "Signal generalises to discovered-buried sites." **(B) Plausible but under-supported.** As argued in Major Concern #3, this is weak generalization within a single, narrow site class. It does not generalize to unknown site types.
*   **Claim:** Implicit claim that this method can probe an "invisible civilization" or "archaeologically erased" record. **(D) Unfalsifiable** and **(C) Overreach.** This is a narrative imposed on the data, not derived from it. The training data actively contradicts this claim.
*   **Claim:** The pipeline is a valid tool for proposing "top-K candidate locations for field verification." **(B) Plausible but under-supported.** It might generate candidates worth checking, but its precision and recall are unknown because the true negatives and false negatives in the landscape are undefined. It could efficiently lead field archaeologists to more temple sites, but that is a modest claim.

**6. WHAT THE PAPER DOES WELL**
1.  **Technical Transparency:** The manuscript is commendably detailed about its computational process, feature extraction, and model parameters. This allows for precise critique and potential replication.
2.  **Acknowledgment of Limitations:** Section 5 correctly identifies key technical limitations (small N, incomplete pipeline, coverage bias). However, it fails to acknowledge the profound *archaeological* limitations.
3.  **Demonstration of a Workflow:** It successfully demonstrates a complete technical workflow from data ingestion to model training and spatial prediction, which is of interest to the computational remote sensing community.

**7. WHAT THE PAPER SHOULD BE, IF NOT THIS**
A modest and publishable paper would:
*   **Reframe the objective:** Present the work as a *site location modeling exercise for Hindu-Buddhist monumental architecture in East Java*. Drop all claims about "invisible," "pre-Hindu," or "erased" civilizations.
*   **Incorporate archaeological variables:** Integrate known archaeological covariates (distance to water, slope, elevation, specific soil types from maps) into the model alongside spectral features. This would ground it in established settlement pattern research.
*   **Conduct a proper blind test:** Define a geographically distinct "validation province" with known but withheld temple sites. Train the model on the rest of Java and see if it can locate the withheld temples. Report precision and recall.
*   **Focus on efficiency:** Argue that the method can prioritize areas for pedestrian survey within large, unsurveyed basins, potentially reducing search costs. Make no claims about direct detection of buried features without coupled geophysical validation.

**8. ONE QUESTION FOR THE AUTHORS**
If the spectral signal is indeed detecting the presence of buried masonry from 4-7m depth, what is the proposed physical mechanism? Please provide a referenced, geophysical model explaining how the mineralogical or moisture contrast of a buried stone structure, through multiple meters of volcanic overburden, consistently affects the surface reflectance captured in 10m pixel dry-season NDWI/NDVI values, and how this model distinguishes that signal from the myriad other sub-surface and surface factors influencing those indices (e.g., soil moisture variation, root density, modern compaction).

**Summary for Editor:**
This referee recommends rejection. The manuscript presents a technically sound but archaeologically naive machine learning application. Its fatal flaw is the complete disconnect between its training data (exclusively Hindu-Buddhist sites) and its interpretive goal (detecting a hypothetical pre-Hindu civilization). The model cannot possibly achieve its stated aim. The methodological validation is circular, the controls are inadequate, and the central claims are overreaching and unsupported. The paper confuses correlation with causation, likely mistaking modern land-use patterns around known sites for a direct spectral signature of buried archaeology. While the computational workflow is clear, its application is based on a fundamental misunderstanding of the archaeological record it seeks to investigate. It should be reframed as a much more modest site location prediction model for a known site class, pending proper archaeological grounding and validation.
