**Referee Report**

**Manuscript Title:** The Invisible Civilisation: Five Independent Lines of Evidence for an Archaeologically Erased Pre-Hindu Nusantara
**Authors:** Mukhlis Amien & Go Frendi Gunawan
**Journal:** *Journal of Anthropological Archaeology*

---

### 1. SUMMARY
The manuscript argues that the apparent absence of pre-4th century CE archaeological sites in the volcanic interior of Java is not evidence of a low population or late state formation, but is instead the product of a "selective survival" taphonomic regime. The authors propose that a substantial pre-Hindu substrate civilization, with a population on the order of 1-2 million people, existed but has been rendered archaeologically invisible primarily due to deep burial by volcanic sedimentation (estimated at ~4.4 mm/yr). This central thesis is supported by the claimed convergence of five independent evidence channels: 1) volcanic sedimentation rates, 2) a demographic gap between predicted and observed settlements, 3) a comparative analysis with the richer pre-400 CE record of the Philippines, 4) linguistic substrate evidence, and 5) a reinterpretation of paleogenomic recovery patterns. The paper's central contribution is a computational, multi-channel argument for systematic archaeological invisibility, culminating in a testable framework and pre-registered predictions.

### 2. OVERALL ASSESSMENT
**Reject.** The core argument is fundamentally unsupported by the evidence presented, relies on a chain of methodological overreaches and flawed assumptions, and proposes a speculative historical scenario that is neither necessary to explain the existing data nor productively testable within the framework of empirical archaeology.

### 3. MAJOR CONCERNS
1.  **The Foundational Premise is a Non-Sequitur.** The paper begins with the observation that monumental stone statues (Dwarapala) are half-buried by volcanic sediment, then leaps to the question of what this process did to "organic architecture." This leap assumes the prior *existence* of that organic architecture. The entire argument is an attempt to prove that existence, but it uses the *potential consequence* of a process (burial) as a *prima facie* argument *for* the existence of the thing being buried. This is circular logic disguised as a geomorphological observation. The sedimentation rate establishes a potential taphonomic filter; it does not, and cannot, provide evidence for what that filter has filtered.

2.  **Demographic Modelling is Presented as Evidence, But is Merely an Assumption.** Section 2, "The Demographic Puzzle," is the engine of the paper, yet its calculations are not evidence of a past reality but exercises in speculative modelling. The "convergence" of three approaches (carrying capacity, back-projection, comparative scaling) is illusory, as all three are built on the same shaky foundation of generalized ethnographic analogy and broad-brush historical estimates. Applying per km² densities from other Austronesian regions to a "habitable area" of 65,000 km² in 400 CE Java is anachronistic and ignores the historical development of Javanese wet-rice intensification, which is the very process that later supported high populations. To argue that a high population *must* have existed in 400 CE because one existed in 1600 CE is a classic teleological error. The "gap" of 1,000- to 7,000-fold is not an empirical finding; it is the arithmetic result of dividing a speculative, model-derived "expected" number by a near-zero "observed" number. The paper treats this manufactured gap as a shocking anomaly requiring a extraordinary explanation, rather than a possible indication that the model's initial parameters are wrong.

3.  **The "Five Independent Channels" are Neither Independent nor Convincing.** The independence claimed in Table 1 is superficial. Channels 1 (sedimentation) and 2 (Philippines comparison) are used to interpret the same demographic "puzzle" from Section 2. Channel 2 is not a controlled experiment. The Philippines and Java differ in countless ways beyond volcanic density and karst availability: tectonic history, soil types, island size and shape, historical trade networks, and, crucially, the intensity and history of archaeological research. To claim the difference in site counts is primarily due to taphonomy is to ignore a century of disciplinary history. The linguistic and genomic channels (3 & 4), only hinted at in this draft, are classic examples of "evidence hunting"—seeking patterns in existing data that can be construed as supportive of a pre-formed theory. Without seeing the full details, the description suggests they will suffer from the same issues: interpreting the absence of open-air ancient DNA in Java as a preservation bias, rather than a reflection of settlement patterns and research focus, is a post-hoc rationalization, not a line of evidence.

4.  **The Argument is Unfalsifiable in Practice.** The paper pays lip service to falsifiability (e.g., deep coring that finds nothing could reject the hypothesis). However, the structure of the argument makes genuine falsification nearly impossible. If a survey fails to find pre-Hindu sites, it is because they are buried too deep (Channel 1). If coring finds nothing, the core was in the wrong location, or the material is too degraded, or the chronology is off. The hypothesis protects itself by invoking an ever-deeper "detection horizon" and a "selective survival" regime that explains both presence and absence. This is not a strength but a critical weakness. A theory that explains everything explains nothing.

5.  **It Dismisses Alternative Explanations Without Engagement.** The paper sets up a strawman: the "conventional narrative" treats the silence as a "chronological fact" of late development. This is a caricature. Southeast Asian archaeologists are deeply engaged with the problems of tropical taphonomy, survey bias, and the visibility of early complex societies. The work of scholars like John Miksic, Pierre-Yves Manguin, and others is reduced to a simplistic "developmental" label. The possibility that early Javanese societies were less centralized, less committed to durable material markers of power, and/or spatially organized in ways not yet identified by survey is not seriously considered. The authors' model-driven approach reverses proper methodology: instead of letting the archaeological record constrain theory, they insist the record must be wrong because it contradicts their model.

### 4. METHODOLOGICAL CONCERNS
*   **Statistical & Comparative Methods:** The demographic modelling lacks any sensitivity analysis that seriously challenges its own premises. What if early Javanese population density was an order of magnitude lower than the ethnographic analogues suggest? What if "habitable area" was far smaller due to technological or social constraints? These alternatives are dismissed out of hand. The Philippines comparison commits the error of comparing a raw site count (275-340) from one region against a count of *known, published* sites (zero) from another, without any normalization for research intensity, area surveyed, or definition of "site." This is not a controlled comparison; it is a juxtaposition of incomparable data.
*   **Hidden Dependence:** The channels are dependent on the shared, unverified assumption of a large pre-400 CE population. Channel 2 uses the demographic "gap" from Section 2 as its motivating puzzle. Channel 1 is used to explain the absence that creates that gap. They are pieces of a circular argument, not independent legs of a stool.
*   **Falsification Criterion:** As noted, the proposed tests (deep coring) are practically and financially prohibitive at the scale needed to be statistically meaningful. The "pre-registered predictions" are likely to be either untestable or so vague that any outcome can be interpreted as supportive.

### 5. SPECIFIC CLAIMS TO CHALLENGE
*   **Claim:** "Java at 400 CE supported roughly one to two million inhabitants." **Assessment: (c) Overreach.** Extrapolated from inappropriate analogues and backward projection from a much later period.
*   **Claim:** The 1,000-7,000 fold gap between expected and observed settlements is a "puzzle" requiring a taphonomic solution. **Assessment: (c) Overreach.** The "puzzle" is an artifact of the model. The gap is primarily in the model, not in reality.
*   **Claim:** The contrast between zero pre-400 CE open-air sites in volcanic Java and hundreds in the Philippines is strong evidence for Java-specific taphonomic destruction. **Assessment: (b) Plausible but under-supported.** Taphonomy is a factor, but the claim is wildly under-supported as the *primary* explanation, as it ignores vast differences in research history, environmental variability, and pre-colonial socio-political trajectories.
*   **Claim:** The five channels "converge" on the selective survival thesis. **Assessment: (d) Unfalsifiable / (c) Overreach.** The convergence is manufactured by interpreting all data through the lens of the desired conclusion. Independence is not achieved.

### 6. WHAT THE PAPER DOES WELL
1.  It forcefully raises a legitimate, if well-known, issue: the visibility of early complex societies in tropical, volcanic environments is a serious challenge for archaeology.
2.  The compilation of volcanic sedimentation rates from archaeological contexts (P1-core) is a useful piece of geoarchaeological synthesis, if published with appropriate caution about its limitations and not leveraged into grand historical claims.
3.  The call for more systematic, subsurface investigation in Java's interior is valid, though the justification provided here is flawed.

### 7. WHAT THE PAPER SHOULD BE, IF NOT THIS
A modest and publishable paper would focus solely on **Channel 1: The Volcanic Sedimentation Rate Calibration.** This paper should:
*   Present the data from the four calibration sites and the 51 eruption-site pairs rigorously.
*   Discuss the implications for archaeological survey methodology in volcanic Java, explicitly stating that the depth of the "detection horizon" means surface survey is blind to earlier periods.
*   **Stop there.** It should *not* argue that this proves a hidden civilization. It should conclude that testing for earlier occupation requires targeted subsurface methods (coring, GPR where feasible, monitoring of deep exposures), and that the current surface record cannot be used to argue for the *absence* of such occupation. This is a significant methodological contribution without the speculative historical overlay.

### 8. ONE QUESTION FOR THE AUTHORS
What specific, concrete archaeological evidence (e.g., a pottery type, a settlement pattern, a ritual complex) would you expect this pre-Hindu substrate civilization to have produced, and how does that expected signature differ from the material culture of the contemporary Buni culture or other known early ISEA societies, such that its *total absence* in the surface and cave record of volcanic Java is explained solely by deep burial and not by cultural or historical factors?

---

**Summary for Editor:** This manuscript proposes a radical reinterpretation of Javanese prehistory, arguing for a large, "invisible" pre-Hindu civilization erased by volcanic sedimentation. While addressing an important taphonomic problem, the argument is fundamentally flawed. It is built on circular logic, speculative demographic modeling that is treated as fact, and a misrepresentation of "independent" lines of evidence that are actually interdependent and interpreted through a biased lens. The core thesis is effectively unfalsifiable and dismisses more parsimonious explanations for the archaeological record. The sole element of value is the synthesis of volcanic sedimentation rates, which could form the basis for a strong methodological paper if stripped of its grand historical claims. As presented, the manuscript is not suitable for publication in a rigorous archaeological journal. I recommend rejection.
