**REFEREE REPORT**

**Manuscript Title:** Why Java's Oldest Kingdoms Appear to Be Elsewhere: Cumulative Volcanic Burial as a Candidate Detection Horizon in Indonesian Archaeology
**Authors:** Mukhlis Amien, Go Frendi Gunawan
**Journal:** *Archaeological Research in Asia*

---

### 1. SUMMARY
The paper argues that the apparent absence of pre-10th century CE archaeological surface evidence in volcanic Java is a taphonomic artefact, not a historical reality. It posits that cumulative volcanic sedimentation, at rates of 2.4–6.2 mm/yr derived from four buried Hindu-Buddhist monuments, has buried pre-Hindu (c. 400 CE) surfaces at depths of 4–10 meters, placing them beyond the reach of standard surface survey and near the limit of geophysical prospection. The central contribution is a geomorphological framework and a proposed research program (OSL dating, tephrochronology, targeted coring) to test this "detection horizon" hypothesis. The paper explicitly frames itself as a provocative "mobilization tool" to spur fieldwork, not as a definitive empirical calibration.

### 2. OVERALL ASSESSMENT
**Major revision.** The core hypothesis is provocative and potentially significant, but the manuscript in its current form is an unstable hybrid of a speculative think-piece and a quantitative research article. The methodological and evidentiary foundations are too weak to support the sweeping historical implications gestured at in the title and introduction. Substantial restructuring and a major tempering of claims are required before it can be considered for publication.

### 3. MAJOR CONCERNS
1.  **Fatal Overreach Between Data and Conclusion (Throughout, but especially Abstract, Introduction, Conclusion):** The paper's title and rhetorical framing ("Why Java's Oldest Kingdoms Appear to Be Elsewhere") imply an answer to a historical question. The data presented—four burial rates for 9th–13th century CE stone monuments—cannot logically support conclusions about the presence, nature, or complexity of *pre-Hindu* societies (c. 400 CE and earlier). This is a fundamental category error. The authors acknowledge this in §5.5 ("we make no claim..."), yet the paper's entire narrative thrust contradicts this disclaimer. The argument leaps from a plausible taphonomic process affecting *monumental archaeology* to an implicit claim about the *systematic erasure of an earlier civilization*. This is unsupported and damagingly speculative.
    *   **Why it matters:** It undermines the paper's scientific credibility and will invite justified criticism from regional specialists. It confuses a testable geomorphological hypothesis with an unfalsifiable historical narrative.
    *   **How to address:** The paper must be radically reframed. The title and abstract must focus exclusively on the taphonomic process and its implications for detecting *the archaeological record of known periods* (e.g., 8th–10th century CE). All speculative language about "invisible civilizations," "pre-Hindu Nusantara," or the primacy of Kutai must be removed. The conclusion should be about detection probabilities, not historical revisionism.

2.  **The "Natural Experiment" is Ill-Specified and Confounded (§1, §4.4):** The comparison between Kutai (non-volcanic) and Java (volcanic) is presented as a clean natural experiment. It is not. The contrast confounds countless variables: differing environmental contexts (riverine vs. volcanic foothills), raw material availability (stone for inscriptions), political and economic structures, research histories, and site formation processes beyond volcanism. To claim the contrast is "difficult to explain without a taphonomic mechanism" (§6) is an extraordinary simplification. The presence of Kutai's surface inscriptions does not make Java's lack of them anomalous; it merely highlights a difference that requires explanation, for which volcanism is only one candidate.
    *   **Why it matters:** The paper's foundational logic is flawed. A weak analogy is being used to motivate a grand hypothesis.
    *   **How to address:** Frame the Kutai-Java contrast as an observation that *motivates a question*, not as proof of a process. Explicitly list the multiple competing hypotheses (e.g., differential investment in durable monuments, political fragmentation, research bias) that could explain the pattern. Position the volcanic burial hypothesis as one testable contender among them.

3.  **The Evidentiary Core is Anecdotal and Non-Representative (§3.2, §3.3, §5.6):** The entire quantitative framework rests on four data points: three accidentally discovered temples and a qualitative colonial description of statues being "half buried." The authors are commendably honest about the limitations (§5.6), but honesty does not compensate for fatal weakness. The Dwarapala measurement is not an "anchor"; it is an unverified anecdote. Using it to derive a precise rate (3.5 mm/yr) and then taking a mean with other rates is statistically meaningless. Furthermore, these are all cases of *monuments*. Their burial dynamics are not analogous to open settlements: their construction often involved digging foundations into existing ground, creating depressions that trap sediment. The derived rates likely overestimate regional aggradation.
    *   **Why it matters:** The proposed "detection horizon" of 4–10m for 400 CE sites is built on a house of cards. It is a projection from a non-representative, shaky baseline.
    *   **How to address:** The paper must retreat from presenting calculated rates and depth projections as results. These should be recast as *illustrative scenarios* based on *maximum possible estimates* from extreme cases. The focus must shift entirely to the research program needed to obtain real data.

4.  **The "51-pair literature compilation" is an Opaque and Potentially Circular Validation (§3.5, §4.2):** This dataset is described in vague terms ("86\% of the evidence was primary") and withheld. Its purpose seems to be to affirm the order-of-magnitude of the anchor rates. However, if it is drawn from the same colonial/volcanological literature, it suffers from the same monumental bias and is not an independent test. This is "validation" by circular sampling.
    *   **Why it matters:** It creates an illusion of cross-validation without substance.
    *   **How to address:** Either publish the full dataset with explicit methodology for inclusion criteria and primary source evaluation as a *supplementary file now*, or remove this section entirely. It currently adds no credible weight to the argument.

5.  **Dismissal of Surface Distribution is Premature and Overstated (§4.4):** The claim that the surface distribution of sites "cannot tell us" anything about past settlement is an overcorrection. While severely biased, the distribution of 9th–15th century stone monuments is not random noise. It reflects, however imperfectly, the core areas of economic and political activity of those kingdoms. To dismiss it entirely because it doesn't fit the desired pre-Hindu narrative is poor scholarship. A more nuanced analysis would ask: given the proposed burial rates, what *should* the surface visibility of 13th-century Majapahit sites be? If the model predicts near-total burial but we find hundreds of surface sites, the model's parameters are wrong.
    *   **Why it matters:** It ignores a potential source of model falsification within the existing data.
    *   **How to address:** Incorporate the known surface record as a weak but relevant test. A simple model: if the mean burial rate is ~4 mm/yr, a 13th-century site should be buried under ~3 meters. The fact that so many are not suggests either (a) the rate is highly spatially variable, (b) monuments were built on elevated platforms, or (c) active maintenance cleared sediment. Engaging with this contradiction would strengthen the paper.

### 4. METHODOLOGICAL CONCERNS
*   **Statistical Methods:** Calculating a mean and standard deviation from n=4 non-independent, monument-derived rates is inappropriate and misleading. The error bars in Fig. 3 imply a precision that does not exist. This should be replaced with a simple statement of the observed range.
*   **Evidence Channels:** The primary anchors and the 51-pair compilation are not independent lines of evidence. They are the same class of evidence (historical reports of monument burial) sampled twice.
*   **Comparative Controls:** The comparisons (Kutai, Philippines, Japan mentioned in background) are not controlled. They are anecdotal analogies, not structured comparative cases accounting for eruption magnitude, soil chemistry, preservation conditions, or research intensity.
*   **Falsifiability:** The hypothesis, as stated in its grand form, is nearly unfalsifiable. A failure to find deeply buried pre-Hindu sites can always be attributed to not looking in the right place, at the right depth, or with the right method. The research program in §5.7, however, contains excellent *falsifiable elements*: OSL dating in basins should yield quantifiable aggradation rates. If those rates are <1 mm/yr, the core hypothesis collapses. This is the paper's strength and should be its centerpiece.
*   **Reproducibility:** The archaeological site dataset from OSM/Wikidata is reproducible in principle but of questionable utility for this specific research question, as the authors admit. The more important lack of reproducibility lies in the unpublished 51-pair compilation.

### 5. SPECIFIC CLAIMS TO CHALLENGE
*   **Claim:** "A Yupa-scale inscribed pillar positioned beside Kelud in 400~CE would now require excavation to be read." **(c) Overreach.** This is a speculative projection based on an unverified rate from 800 years later.
*   **Claim:** "The surface distribution... cannot be used to argue for past cultural absence unless the detection horizon is first characterised." **(b) Plausible but under-supported.** The principle is sound (taphonomy matters), but the paper does not provide the characterized horizon it calls for. It only provides a speculative estimate.
*   **Claim:** The Kutai-Java contrast is "difficult to explain without a taphonomic mechanism." **(d) Unfalsifiable / (c) Overreach.** It is easily explained by multiple mechanisms; this claim dismisses them without engagement.
*   **Claim:** Liangan "demonstrates that the burial regime... can preserve organic architecture." **(a) Well-supported.** This is a valid and important point. Liangan is a crucial data point for preservation potential, though it represents catastrophic, not cumulative, burial.
*   **Claim:** The proposed research program can "upgrade preliminary anchors to a validated Java-wide rate." **(b) Plausible but under-supported.** The methods are appropriate, but the scale and challenge of obtaining a "Java-wide" rate are immense and understated.

### 6. WHAT THE PAPER DOES WELL
1.  **Identifies a Genuine Problem:** The paper forcefully highlights a potentially massive taphonomic bias in Javanese archaeology that is often overlooked. The basic premise that cumulative volcanism buries sites is incontrovertible; the question of *how much* and *with what effect on survey* is worthy of study.
2.  **Proposes a Concrete, Doable Research Program (§5.7):** This is the best part of the paper. The suggestions for OSL dating, tephrochronology, and targeted coring are specific, methodologically sound, and feasible. They shift the discussion from speculation to actionable science.
3.  **Uses Liangan Effectively:** The Liangan case study is a powerful example of exceptional preservation under tephra and is used correctly to counter the argument that organic remains would not survive.

### 7. WHAT THE PAPER SHOULD BE, IF NOT THIS
This paper should be a **short, provocative "Research Prospectus" or "Forum Article."** It should:
*   **Title:** Refocus on the methodological challenge. E.g., "The Volcanic Detection Horizon: A Geoarchaeological Challenge in Javanese Archaeology."
*   **Content:** Jettison all quantitative projections based on the four monuments. Instead, use them as *illustrative examples* of the process. Present the Kutai-Java contrast as a *puzzle*. Spend 80% of the paper detailing and justifying the proposed research program (§5.7), explicitly framing it as a necessary step before any historical inference can be made. Acknowledge competing hypotheses for the surface record pattern.
*   **Goal:** The paper's value is in agenda-setting and methodology-proposing, not in presenting findings. It should end with a clear call for collaborative geoarchaeological work.

### 8. ONE QUESTION FOR THE AUTHORS
If your proposed OSL and coring program were carried out in the Malang basin and returned a landscape-scale aggradation rate of 0.8 mm/yr (i.e., an order of magnitude lower than your monument-based estimates), would you consider your central hypothesis—that cumulative volcanic burial explains the lack of pre-10th century surface evidence—to be falsified?
