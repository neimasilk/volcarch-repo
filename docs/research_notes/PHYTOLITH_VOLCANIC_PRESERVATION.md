## Executive Summary

**Can silica phytoliths survive volcanic burial in Java?** YES. The evidence is overwhelming:
- Phytoliths survive **90,000 years** in volcanic tephra sequences (Aso, Japan)
- Phytoliths survive under the **74,000-year-old Toba super-eruption** ash (India)

**If rice phytoliths are recovered from pre-400 CE volcanic deposits in Java, it would be the single strongest piece of evidence for VOLCARCH's core thesis.**

## The Science

### What Are Phytoliths?
Microscopic bodies of opaline silica (SiO2·nH2O) formed within plant cells. Plants absorb dissolved silica from soil; it precipitates as amorphous opal-A in cell walls and lumina. Phytoliths retain the morphology of the cells they formed in, making them identifiable to genus and sometimes species level after the organic material decays.

### What Preserves Them?
- Acidic to neutral pH soils (pH < 8) — FAVOURABLE
- Dry, aerobic conditions
- Burial under sediment (reduces biological cycling)
- Among the **most durable** plant microfossils — survive fire, decomposition, geological time

### What Destroys Them?
- **Alkaline conditions (pH > 9)** — primary chemical threat. Dissolution accelerates sharply above pH 8.
- Differential dissolution by morphotype: thin-walled types dissolve faster than compact thick-walled types
- Biological cycling in surface soils (1-18 month residence time) — but burial escapes this

## Evidence: Phytoliths Survive Volcanic Burial

| Study | Context | Age | Finding |
|-------|---------|-----|---------|
| **Miyabuchi & Sugiyama 2011, 2015** | Aso Volcano, Japan | **90,000 years** | Continuous phytolith record through dozens of tephra layers. Grass/bamboo vegetation identified. |
| **Petraglia et al. 2012** | Jurreru Valley, India (under Toba ash) | **74,000 years** | Phytoliths recovered from 10 cm beneath Youngest Toba Tuff. C3/C4 vegetation mosaic. |
| **Opfergelt et al.** | Mt Pinatubo, Philippines (1991) | ~30 years + 500-yr paleosols | Phytolith accumulation in new soils on pyroclastic deposits. Buried paleosols also preserved. |
| **Inoue et al. 2011** | Unzen Volcano, Kyushu | Late Pleistocene | Phytoliths in buried humic soils within tephra-soil sequences. |

**Conclusion:** Phytoliths survive volcanic burial over geological timescales (90,000+ years). The VOLCARCH target period (1,600-2,500 years for pre-400 CE) is well within demonstrated survival.

## pH Analysis for Java

| Factor | pH | Implication |
|--------|-----|-------------|
| Fresh andesitic ash (typical Java) | 8.5-8.9 | Below critical pH 9 threshold — moderate risk |
| Fresh ash surface coatings (acid gases) | 4-6 temporarily | Paradoxically protective |
| Weathered andisol (years-decades) | 5-7 | **Excellent preservation** |
| Mature allophanic andisol (centuries+) | 5.5-6.5 | **Long-term stable** |
| **Critical dissolution threshold** | **> 9** | **NOT reached by Javanese deposits** |

**Trajectory:** Fresh Javanese tephra starts near pH 8.5-8.9 → weathers downward to pH 5-7. Phytoliths face a **brief window** (days-weeks) of elevated but sub-critical dissolution risk, then enter long-term favourable conditions. The 90,000-year Aso record proves this trajectory is survivable.

**Key protection:** Phytoliths already in **paleosols** beneath new tephra deposits **never experience** the alkaline phase — they are sealed in their original soil chemistry.

## Rice Phytolith Identification

Rice (*Oryza sativa*) produces multiple diagnostic phytolith types:

1. **Double-peaked glume cells** (husk) — diagnostic to genus *Oryza*, identifiable "with high certainty" (Zhao et al. 1998)
2. **Bulliform (fan-shaped) phytoliths** (leaves) — diagnostic to genus. "Fish-scale" surface decoration distinguishes wild from domesticated rice: **≥9 fish-scale decorations = domesticated** (Zhang et al. 2019)

**Existing Indonesian evidence:**
- **Minanga Sipakko, Sulawesi** — rice phytoliths confirmed agriculture by 3,500 BP (Deng et al. 2020, *Scientific Reports*). Earliest validated phytolith evidence for rice farming in Indonesia.
- **South Sulawesi** — *Oryza* husk phytoliths from early palace sites (Bulbeck & Caldwell 2008)
- **Liangan, Java** — carbonised rice grains (tropical japonica, Castillo 2014) but **NO systematic phytolith extraction from volcanic matrix**

**If NOT found:** Could mean (a) agriculture wasn't present (Counter 1 wins), (b) phytoliths dissolved (unlikely given pH evidence), or (c) sampling was insufficient.

## Practical Pathway

### Method
1. Obtain volcanic core samples from Java (PVMBG monitoring cores, construction boreholes, or dedicated coring)
2. Standard phytolith extraction: heavy liquid flotation (sodium polytungstate, SG ~2.3) + oxidation
3. **Challenge:** volcanic glass (cryptotephra) has similar density to phytoliths → co-separates during flotation
4. **Solution:** optical microscopy distinguishes amorphous glass from structured phytolith morphology (Lentfer & Boyd 2000 protocol for volcanic soils)
5. Identify rice phytoliths using Zhao/Pearsall/Zhang criteria

### Target Samples
- **Best:** PVMBG monitoring cores from volcanic flanks in Central/East Java (Merapi, Kelud, Sindoro systems)
- **Alternative:** Liangan excavation matrix (volcanic deposits sealing the settlement — phytoliths from pre-eruption agricultural surfaces)
- **Alternative:** Construction borehole samples from volcanic interior Java

### Collaboration Partners
- **Cristina Castillo** (UCL) — already worked on Liangan rice, leading archaeobotanist for SE Asia rice
- **Balai Arkeologi Yogyakarta** (Sugeng Riyanto) — Liangan excavation team
- **PVMBG** — volcanic monitoring cores
- **Dolores Piperno** (Smithsonian) — phytolith methodology
- **Zhenhua Deng** — validated rice phytolith method for Indonesia (Sulawesi work)

### Budget
- Phytolith extraction and analysis: relatively low cost (standard laboratory procedure)
- Core samples: free if piggybacking on PVMBG monitoring programs
- **Could be proposed as a collaborative research project** — low cost, high impact
