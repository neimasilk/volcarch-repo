RUNNINGHEAD: Uncoded basic vocabulary in six Sulawesi word lists
AUTHOR: Mukhlis Amien | Universitas Bhinneka Nusantara
AUTHOR: Go Frendi Gunawan | Universitas Bhinneka Nusantara
KEYWORDS: Sulawesi languages, Austronesian Basic Vocabulary Database, cognate coding, Makasar, Tolaki, substrate, classification
CITED: greenhill2008, forkel2018, gray2009, list2012, blust2013, sneddon1993, vandenberg1996demise, mead2003, mills1975diss, sirk1989, bulbeck1992, bulbeck2000, bellwood1997, blust2012ligature, reid1994, nahm2022, white2023, chen2016, breiman2001, pedregosa2011, lundberg2017, levenshtein1966, cohen1960, anderson2018
ABSTRACT: In the Austronesian Basic Vocabulary Database (ABVD), part of every Sulawesi word list carries no cognate-set assignment, a residue often read as pre-Austronesian substrate. We ask what the absence of a cognate-set number does and does not show, using 1,357 forms of six ABVD lists from four Sulawesi subgroups, 438 of them (32.3 percent) uncoded. A classifier given the written form and the meaning ranks uncoded above coded forms with an area under the curve of 0.73 (0.67 from the form alone) but barely improves on calling every form coded. Uncoded forms are about one character longer, more often carry a written glottal mark and more often denote actions. Makasar has more uncoded meanings than Bugis and Sa'dan Toraja (34.8 against 23.4 and 19.0 percent), and few of them resemble its relatives' forms; 70.9 percent of Tolaki's uncoded forms recur within Bungku-Tolaki. Coding status is not origin: a set number is not a finding of inheritance, nor its absence one of non-Austronesian descent. The ranked lists are released.

# Introduction

In the basic vocabulary of many Austronesian languages some forms cannot be assigned to a higher-level proto-form: no cognate is known in other languages, and the form reconstructs at most to a low-level proto-language or not at all. Such residues have long attracted a particular reading. For three Philippine Negrito languages, Reid (1994) counted forms with no known cognate anywhere at 17 to 29 percent of a 539-item list and, after weighing lost inherited forms, radical sound change and coinage, preferred a non-Austronesian substratum as their source. For Sulawesi the reading has been attached above all to Makasar. Lexicostatistical counts have long shown it as the most divergent of the South Sulawesi languages (Mills 1975:491; Sirk 1989:71), a substratum has been named among the possible reasons (Mills 1975; see also Bulbeck 1992), and Bulbeck, Pasqua and Di Lello (2000:103), citing a retention figure of 38 percent from Proto-Malayo-Polynesian, describe the remaining 62 percent of the basic vocabulary as open to investigation as borrowing from pre-Austronesian, possibly Toalean, languages.

The Austronesian Basic Vocabulary Database (ABVD; Greenhill, Blust and Gray 2008), in its Cross-Linguistic Data Formats version (Forkel et al. 2018), records for every form of a 210-meaning list whether its editors have grouped it with forms in other lists under a cognate-set number. The forms left without a number are the operational residue of the comparative method as it has been applied within one database. As computational work on Austronesian lexical data has grown (Gray, Drummond and Greenhill 2009; List 2012; Forkel et al. 2018), that residue is easy to extract and tempting to interpret. This paper asks what it is. For six ABVD word lists from Sulawesi we ask three things: whether uncoded forms can be told apart from coded ones by their written shape and their meaning; what, measurably, distinguishes them; and what the absence of a cognate-set number means in the two lists where the question matters most, Makasar and Tolaki.

Sulawesi is a suitable place to ask. Blust (2013:82) lists eleven linguistic microgroups for the island and notes some evidence for a larger Celebic grouping; Sneddon (1993:2) counts nine microgroups and adds Banggai as a group of one. A Celebic supergroup containing Bungku-Tolaki, Muna-Buton and Wotu-Wolio, but not South Sulawesi, has been proposed with stated reservations (van den Berg 1996; Mead 2003). Our six lists represent four of these groups: South Sulawesi (Bugis, Makasar, Sa'dan Toraja), Muna-Buton (Muna), Wotu-Wolio (Wolio) and Bungku-Tolaki (Tolaki). Three of the four fall inside the proposed Celebic supergroup, a point we return to when similarity across lists is discussed.

Two terms are used throughout. A form is *coded* when it carries at least one cognate-set number in the ABVD snapshot we used, and *uncoded* when it carries none. By the *profile* of the uncoded forms we mean the set of measurable properties of their written shape and of their meaning that distinguish them, in these lists, from the coded forms. The profile is part orthographic and part semantic, and it is operationalised by a statistical classifier; it is not a phonological fingerprint, because the inputs are written forms, not phonemic transcriptions, and because some of the inputs describe the meaning.

The paper makes no claim about the origin of any form. Where an etymological judgement would be needed, none of the authors can supply it, and the paper says so. What stands in its place is a set of mechanical comparisons with explicit controls for chance: a classifier assessed on forms and on whole lists it has not seen; a look-alike screen whose sensitivity is measured on forms the database itself has coded; a permutation test whose decision rule was written before it was run. Each of these can be repeated by anyone with the released files, and each has limits that are stated where the result is given. Section 2 describes the data, the label, the inputs and the tests; Section 3 gives the results in eight short parts; Section 4 discusses what they do and do not show; Section 5 concludes.

# Data and methods

## Data

The data are six word lists of the ABVD, in the lexibank/abvd CLDF release at repository state 917c5a5 (7 October 2025; licence CC BY 4.0): Muna (ABVD list 27; 219 forms; compiled from van den Berg), Bugis (48; 242 forms; Soppeng dialect, compiled by Zainuddin Taha), Makasar (166; 217 forms; Abd. Rajab), Wolio (192; 254 forms; J. C. Anceaux), Sa'dan Toraja (226; 216 forms; ABVD's list "Tae' (S. Toraja)", entered by Blust from van der Veen's dictionary of 1940) and Tolaki (674; 209 forms; Omar Abdullah Pidani). We use the spelling *Makasar* and the name *Sa'dan Toraja* in the text; the tables keep ABVD's identifiers so that every row can be traced.

ABVD's meaning list has 210 items. The six lists cover between 200 and 210 of them, and a meaning can have more than one form in a list: 117 of the 1,357 rows are a second or third form for their meaning. Every count in this paper is therefore a count of forms, with one exception noted in Section 2.6, where the Makasar comparison is made by meaning. The 210-item list is ABVD's own, and we do not call it a Swadesh list. For the computation of the inputs described in Section 2.3, bracketed additions by the compiler, leading and trailing punctuation and stray spaces were removed from the written form; the released files give the form exactly as ABVD records it.

## The label

A form counts as *coded* if its Cognacy field in the snapshot holds at least one cognate-set number, and as *uncoded* if the field is empty. By this definition 919 forms (67.7 percent) are coded and 438 (32.3 percent) are uncoded. This is the only label used in the paper. An earlier version of this study used two label sets and a step that was meant to recover inherited forms by comparison with reconstructions; the step compared no forms with any reconstruction and has been removed, as has a filter for loanwords that matched five unrelated words.

Two things about this label must be said at the outset, because the rest of the paper depends on them. First, a cognate-set number records that ABVD's editors grouped a form with other forms. It is not a finding of inheritance. ABVD flags loans only occasionally; eleven forms in these six lists carry its loan flag (Muna 2, Bugis 1, Makasar 1, Wolio 2, Tolaki 5, none in the Sa'dan Toraja list), and five of the eleven also carry a cognate-set number. The clearest case is Tolaki *kila* 'lightning', flagged by ABVD as a loan from Malay, which sits in the same set as the Proto-Malayo-Polynesian entry. Sets also differ greatly in breadth: the median coded form in the Bugis list belongs to a set attested in 284 lists, the median coded Muna form to a set attested in 54, and 35.7 percent of Muna's coded forms belong to sets of five lists or fewer. Second, how much of a list is coded depends on what else happens to be in the database. Of the 185 coded Muna forms, 44 are coded only because the same form recurs in ABVD's second Muna list (Wuna, list 147, which shares Muna's Glottolog code); counted as uncoded they would raise Muna's uncoded share from 15.5 to 35.6 percent. The five Tolaki dialect lists in ABVD are not cross-coded with the Tolaki list in this way (no form). The label is thus not the same kind of thing in every list, and "uncoded" is not an upper bound on anything: a form of non-Austronesian or borrowed origin can carry a set number, and an inherited form can lack one.

## Inputs of the classifier

Twenty-five inputs are computed for every form, seventeen from the written form and eight from the meaning. We name them by what the code computes rather than by what the measurement might be taken to mean. From the written form: length in characters (hyphens, spaces and glottal marks included); the number and the share of vowel letters; whether the form ends in a vowel letter; whether it contains a written glottal mark (ʔ or an apostrophe); whether it contains ŋ or the digraph *ng*, or one of the letter pairs *mb, nd, nj, mp, nk, nc, nt*; whether it contains a hyphen or the same block of two or three letters twice in a row; the number of consonant-letter clusters; whether it begins with one of the strings *ma, me, mo, pa, ka, ta, na, po* or *aŋ*; and eight indicators for the first letter (*a, b, k, m, p, s, t*, other). The code's own names for some of these inputs ("nasal cluster", "reduplication", "prefix-like") say more than the inputs measure: of the 394 forms that begin with one of the nine strings, 88 have four letters or fewer (for instance *mata*, *tau*), and of the 142 forms flagged for a hyphen or a repeated block, 93 are flagged for the hyphen alone, almost all of them in the Bugis and Sa'dan Toraja lists, where the hyphen appears to mark a boundary set by the compiler. We therefore speak of an onset string, of a nasal input and of a hyphen-or-repetition input, and we test stricter versions of each in Section 3.5.

From the meaning: one indicator for membership in a core set of 174 of the 210 meanings, and seven indicators for broad semantic domains (action, body, nature, quality, number, grammatical word, other). The core set and the domains are groupings made by the authors for this study; they are not taken from Concepticon or from the World Loanword Database, and the core set is not the Swadesh 100-item list. The classifier has no input that identifies the source list. An earlier version used such an input, an integer that numbered the six lists; it was the strongest input of that model and has been removed. Table 5 lists the inputs that matter with their observed values.

## The classifier

The main classifier is gradient-boosted decision trees (XGBoost; Chen and Guestrin 2016), with 300 trees of depth four and otherwise default settings, trained to separate uncoded from coded forms; the score it gives a form is its estimated probability of belonging to the uncoded class, so a higher score means a form that looks more like the uncoded forms. A random forest (Breiman 2001) and a logistic regression, both from scikit-learn (Pedregosa et al. 2011), were run on the same inputs to see whether the result depends on the choice of method. No class weighting was applied. The size of each input's contribution to the predictions is summarised by the mean absolute SHAP value (Lundberg and Lee 2017), shown in Figure 1. Software: Python 3.11, scikit-learn 1.8.0, xgboost 3.0.3, shap 0.50.0.

## How the classifier is assessed

A classifier that is scored on the forms it was trained on will look better than it is. We therefore report two kinds of out-of-sample assessment. In cross-validation the 1,357 forms are divided at random into five parts; a model trained on four parts scores the fifth; this is done for every part, and the whole procedure is repeated ten times with different divisions, giving fifty test sets. In the held-out-list assessment the model is trained on five lists and scores the sixth, once for each list. The second assessment is what "generalises across Sulawesi languages" means concretely: a model that has never seen a Tolaki form is asked to rank the Tolaki forms.

The main measure is the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC): the probability that a randomly chosen uncoded form receives a higher score than a randomly chosen coded form. A value of 0.5 is chance and 1.0 is perfect ranking. Verbal scales for the AUC exist (for instance Nahm 2022, Table 4, which calls 0.7 to 0.8 "fair" and 0.6 to 0.7 "poor"), but they are conventions without an empirical basis (White et al. 2023:2), and we report the numbers rather than the labels. We also report accuracy, the share of forms placed in the right class at a score threshold of 0.5, together with the accuracy of the trivial rule that calls every form coded (0.677), and the precision, recall and F1 score of the uncoded class. Two further baselines matter: a model given only the identity of the source list reaches an AUC of 0.680, and a model given only the eight meaning inputs reaches 0.646.

Variation is reported as the standard deviation of the AUC over the ten repetition means and over the fifty test sets; these are measures of the stability of the procedure, not confidence intervals. A bootstrap over forms of the pooled out-of-sample scores gives an interval of about plus or minus 0.03 around the main figure. For the held-out-list assessment we also give, for each list, the accuracy of the majority answer for that list, since a classifier can be worse than that answer.

## The three checks and the spelling tests

Three further analyses ask what the label means. (a) *Makasar against Proto-Malayo-Polynesian.* For each meaning that has an entry in ABVD's Proto-Malayo-Polynesian (PMP) list and a form in the Makasar list (201 meanings), the Makasar form is placed in one of three classes: it shares a cognate-set number with the PMP entry; it is coded but in another set; or it is uncoded. The same is done for Bugis, Sa'dan Toraja, Wolio, Muna and Tolaki. The unit here is the meaning, not the form, and the rule for the three classes was fixed before the counts were made. (b) *Tolaki against its subgroup.* Each Tolaki form is compared with the forms of the same meaning in ABVD's Proto-Bungku-Tolaki list (780) and in 42 other Bungku-Tolaki lists (41 of them contributed by one compiler, D. Mead; 13 languages by Glottolog code). The comparison is a look-alike screen: a normalised edit distance (Levenshtein 1966) between the written forms after bracketed material, hyphens, apostrophes and the glottal mark have been removed, computed so that up to three leading characters may be ignored on either side, with a match counted at a distance of 0.34 or less (a stricter run uses 0.25). The chance rate is the same comparison made against the forms of other meanings. The screen's sensitivity is measured on forms that ABVD itself places in a cognate set; because ABVD's coders and the screen both work from surface resemblance, that sensitivity is an upper bound on what the screen would find among cognates the coders missed. The same screen is applied to the uncoded Makasar forms, against the PMP entry and against the Bugis and Sa'dan Toraja forms of the same meaning. A look-alike is not a cognate judgement, and a distance of zero does not mean that two forms are identical. (c) *Similarity across lists.* For every meaning, the uncoded forms of different lists are paired; the statistic is the mean normalised distance over all such pairs, compared with its distribution when the meaning labels of the uncoded forms are shuffled within each list (10,000 permutations); the number of look-alike pairs at the 0.34 threshold is counted beside it. Numerals are excluded from the primary set because decimal compounds are transparent across the lists. The coded forms, paired in the same way, serve as a positive control. The decision rule for this test was written before it was run, on the same day and by the same analyst; it was not registered externally.

The spelling tests ask how far the classifier depends on orthographic convention. The written glottal mark was re-coded in six ways (as *q*, as *k*, left unwritten, as a geminate of the following consonant, apostrophe unified to ʔ, and the input removed), the digraphs *ng* and *ny* and the Muna digraphs *gh*, *bh* and *dh* were converted to single symbols, and the two size inputs (length and vowel count) were removed, each time re-running the cross-validation and the held-out-list assessment.

## Makasar

Table 2 gives, for each list, the share of meanings whose form is in the same cognate set as the PMP entry, coded in another set, or uncoded. For Makasar the three shares are 39.3, 25.9 and 34.8 percent of 201 meanings; for Bugis 48.3, 28.4 and 23.4; for Sa'dan Toraja 48.0, 33.0 and 19.0. It is a lower bound on retention, not retention, because an uncoded meaning cannot be in the set whatever its origin; if every uncoded Makasar meaning turned out to belong there, the share would be 74.1 percent. What distinguishes Makasar from its two relatives in these data is one fact, not three. On the meanings shared with each relative, Makasar has more uncoded meanings than Bugis (exact McNemar test, p = 0.006) and than Sa'dan Toraja (p = 0.0001; both unadjusted). Its lower share in the PMP set and its smaller share of cognate sets held in common with each relative (41.4 and 39.2 percent of all meanings, against 53.1 percent between Bugis and Sa'dan Toraja) are the same fact counted again: on the meanings coded in both lists, Makasar is in the PMP set for 70 of 110 meanings against Bugis's 69, and for 70 of 115 against Sa'dan Toraja's 71. Those conditional figures decide nothing about the uncoded forms, because they would be the same whether the uncoded forms are cognates that have not been coded or words that have replaced the inherited ones.

The look-alike screen says a little more. Of 75 uncoded Makasar forms whose meaning has a PMP entry, two resemble the PMP form (about one is expected by chance); the screen finds 38 percent of the Makasar forms that ABVD itself places in the PMP entry's set, and that figure is an upper bound for cognates the coders missed. Of 80 uncoded Makasar forms, 10 (12.5 percent) resemble the Bugis or the Sa'dan Toraja form of the same meaning, against 3.8 percent expected by chance and against 90 of 136 (66 percent) among the coded Makasar forms. The screen therefore does not support the reading that Makasar's uncoded forms are, for the most part, cognates that have simply not been coded; it leaves open what they are. For 28 of the 70 uncoded Makasar meanings the Bugis form is in the PMP set, and for 25 of 69 the Sa'dan Toraja form is; these meanings are listed in the released files for anyone able to judge them.

How does this fit the published figure? The 38 percent cited by Bulbeck, Pasqua and Di Lello (2000:103) comes from Bellwood (1997:115), who reports Blust's lexicostatistical comparison of 62 Austronesian languages with a 200-item reconstructed PMP list: Western Malayo-Polynesian languages retain 41 percent on average, Malay 59, Minangkabau 50, Tagalog 46, Makasar 38, Sundanese 35 and Yogyakarta Javanese 30. Our 39.3 percent is a count of the same kind, in the same tradition (ABVD's PMP list is Blust's), on a different Makasar word list; that the two agree is not independent confirmation of either, and in its source the figure is not presented as low. A more independent comparison is Sirk's (1989:71) lexicostatistical table, made with other word lists and another judge: Makasar shares 45 percent with Bugis and 42 with Sa'dan, while Bugis and Sa'dan share 60. ABVD's cognate coding gives 41.4, 39.2 and 53.1 percent for the same pairs, the same order with gaps of the same size. The data are thus compatible with the lexical divergence of Makasar reported in that literature; they do not establish it, and they say nothing about its cause, whether substratum, borrowing or innovation. (The first reviewer's report gives our earlier figure for Makasar as 30.1 percent; the submitted table printed 30.9.)

## Tolaki

The Tolaki list has a cognate-set number for 75 of its 209 forms (35.9 percent), by far the lowest share of the six. An earlier version of this paper called this under-documentation. That is the wrong word: the list is not short of data. It fills 200 of the 210 meanings, and of its 86 uncoded forms whose meaning is also covered by ABVD's five Tolaki dialect lists, 88.4 percent recur in those lists. What is sparse is the cognate coding of the whole subgroup. ABVD's Proto-Bungku-Tolaki list is coded for 35.6 percent of its forms, and the 42 other Bungku-Tolaki lists for between 29.9 and 59.6 percent (median 45.0). The unit matters here: the share by form falls with the length of a list (Spearman's rho = −0.55), because longer lists carry more synonyms. Counted by meaning, with the first form of each meaning and on the meanings the lists share, the Tolaki list is coded for 38.3 percent, the five dialect lists for 36.1 to 40.6 percent, and the 42 comparison lists for a median of 46.7 percent, five of them at or below the Tolaki figure. The six Tolaki lists sit on the lower side of a thinly coded subgroup, by a moderate margin, among lists almost all of which come from one compiler.

The look-alike screen shows where the uncoded Tolaki forms recur. Of the 134 uncoded forms, 70.9 percent have a look-alike of the same meaning in the Proto-Bungku-Tolaki list or in one of the 42 other lists, against 10.5 percent expected by chance; at the strict threshold the figures are 63.4 against 3.0 percent. For the coded Tolaki forms the same screen finds a look-alike for 95.9 percent (chance 14.6). The uncoded forms of this list are, for the most part, forms that recur within its subgroup. Of the 34 uncoded forms that match an entry of the Proto-Bungku-Tolaki list, seven match an entry that ABVD does assign to a cognate set; these seven are coding gaps in the plain sense. Twelve of the 128 uncoded forms whose meaning has a PMP entry resemble that entry by the screen (about two are expected by chance; nine remain at the strict threshold; one of the twelve is loan-flagged); the screen finds 48 percent of the Tolaki forms that ABVD itself places in the PMP set, so that too is a ceiling. For the remaining forms the study cannot decide between a coding gap, a chance resemblance and a loan.

Recurrence within the subgroup says nothing about origin. The first reviewer's example makes the point: a word borrowed from a non-Austronesian language by the speakers of a low-level proto-language is inherited by all its daughters and recurs across the subgroup exactly as an inherited Austronesian word does. The screen also compares spellings, not sound correspondences, across lists that are largely one compiler's; the twelve PMP look-alikes are listed in the released files, and none of them should be taken as an etymology before a specialist has looked at it.

Three properties stand. Uncoded forms are longer: 6.10 against 5.20 characters, a within-list difference of +1.09 [0.87, 1.32] characters, or +0.93 [0.72, 1.13] counting letters only; the vowel count measures the same thing. They more often carry a written glottal mark: 23.5 against 11.6 percent, odds ratio 2.67 [1.88, 3.79]. The contrast is present in each of the lists that write the mark: Bugis 2.04, Makasar 2.01, Sa'dan Toraja 2.40; in the Tolaki list all 29 marked forms are uncoded and no coded form carries a mark, which pulls the pooled figure up (without Tolaki, 2.11). And they more often have an action meaning: 40.4 against 23.4 percent, odds ratio 2.46 [1.89, 3.20]. With the number of letters held fixed (strata of list by letter count), the glottal mark gives 2.60 [1.79, 3.76] (2.19 [1.50, 3.22] without Tolaki) and the action meaning 2.22 [1.67, 2.95]; consonant-letter clusters give 1.51 [1.09, 2.09], but they overlap with the nasal-plus-stop pairs and with glottal mark plus consonant, and with the glottal mark also held fixed the figure is 1.38 [0.98, 1.93].

Two properties that the earlier version of this paper reported do not stand as stated. The onset-string input (the strings the code calls prefix-like) is more frequent among uncoded forms, 37.0 against 25.2 percent (odds ratio 1.57 [1.20, 2.04]), not less, as we had written. But it is not separable from length: with the number of letters held fixed the odds ratio is 1.09 [0.80, 1.48], and with the number of letters after the matched string held fixed it is 2.51 [1.85, 3.40]. Two ways of equalising length give two answers, so the data neither show fewer onset strings nor establish more; a length criterion alone ("six letters or more") gives an odds ratio of 2.56 [1.98, 3.32]. The nasal input behaves in the same way: 30.4 against 23.7 percent as coded (1.55 [1.18, 2.04]), 21.0 against 12.7 percent for a strict nasal-plus-stop definition (2.05 [1.47, 2.84]), and 0.95 [0.70, 1.30] or 1.24 [0.84, 1.81] with letters held fixed. The hyphen-or-repetition input gives the same estimate under every definition (1.68 as coded; 1.61 [0.87, 2.98] for a repeated block alone; 1.57 [0.94, 2.63] for a hyphen alone), with intervals that include one; it is too thin to stand as a separate property. "Ends in a vowel" is the glottal mark counted a second time: in the three South Sulawesi lists, the only lists with consonant-final forms, uncoded forms are 23.8 percent of the vowel-final forms, 25.9 percent of the forms ending in another consonant and 39.3 percent of the forms ending in a glottal mark; with the glottal-final forms set aside the odds ratio is 0.91 [0.58, 1.44]. The core-set indicator does not differ (1.04 [0.76, 1.43]).

Figure 1 ranks the inputs by their mean absolute contribution to the predictions of the full model. The six largest are the glottal mark (0.279), length (0.276), the action meaning (0.269), the vowel count (0.266), the vowel share (0.210) and consonant-letter clusters (0.198). The figure describes the classifier, not the vocabulary: for "ends in a vowel" the model's direction is the reverse of the within-list association, because all 285 consonant-final forms are in the three South Sulawesi lists, so that input also tells the model which list a form comes from, and because it overlaps with the glottal input. Which of the two accounts for the sign was not tested. Statements about the vocabulary in this paper rest on Table 5, not on Figure 1.

The action meanings deserve a word, because their forms are also longer (5.95 against 5.31 characters, in four of six lists) and are cited differently from list to list: 91.9 percent of the Muna action forms begin with *a*, as do 81.7 percent of the Makasar ones, while 61.7 percent of the Tolaki action forms begin with one of the nine onset strings. A natural reading of the whole bundle is that uncoded forms are more often morphologically complex citation forms, verbs with their affixes. We state it as a reading. No form was segmented in this study, the only compiler-marked segmentation in the data (the hyphen in the Bugis and Sa'dan Toraja lists) gives a mixed result (uncoded forms are 34.4 percent of hyphenated Bugis forms against 22.5 percent of the rest, and 20.0 against 20.9 percent in Sa'dan Toraja), and a rival reading fits the same numbers: affixed citation forms may simply be harder to code, so that the profile would be a profile of coding difficulty rather than of the words. A third reading, that some lists write phonological length or glottalisation where others do not, is taken up in Section 3.6.

Table 6 crosses the label with the model's out-of-sample score at a threshold of 0.5. Of the 438 uncoded forms, 172 score 0.5 or more ("uncoded, profile") and 266 below it; of the 919 coded forms, 105 score 0.5 or more and 814 below. The agreement between label and score is kappa = 0.31 (Cohen 1960). An earlier version of this paper reported a kappa of 0.61 and a set of 266 forms "confirmed by both methods"; that agreement was computed on the forms the model had been trained on and is withdrawn. ## Does the result depend on spelling?

The classifier sees only what the compilers wrote. A glottal stop written as *q* or *k*, or not written at all, is invisible to it, and the lists differ in how they write it: 36 percent of the Makasar forms carry ʔ, 22 percent of the Sa'dan Toraja forms (47 with ʔ, one with an apostrophe), 21 percent of the Bugis forms, 14 percent of the Tolaki forms (29, all with an apostrophe), four Wolio forms (two uncoded, two coded) and no Muna form. Whether these differences are differences of notation or of phonology we cannot say; a language without a phonemic glottal stop has nothing to write, and the question is one for specialists. In the Tolaki list every form written with an apostrophe is uncoded. Eleven Sa'dan Toraja forms end in *k* beside 45 that end in a glottal mark, in one and the same source; since ABVD's PMP entries for two of those eleven meanings themselves end in *k* (\*manuk 'bird', \*tasik 'sea'), we do not treat that *k* as a way of writing the glottal stop. Glottal mark followed by a consonant occurs in 35 forms (Makasar 26, Sa'dan Toraja 7, Bugis 2) and is also counted by the cluster input.

Table 8 gives the AUC under each re-coding of the glottal mark. The six variants are three manipulations. Writing the mark as a consonant letter (*q* or *k*), which is the same as removing the input, costs 0.016 of cross-validated AUC and between 0.020 and 0.024 for the held-out lists; leaving it unwritten costs 0.006; writing the pre-glottalised consonant as a geminate costs 0.007; unifying the apostrophe to ʔ changes no input. The AUC stays at or above 0.710 in every variant (held-out lists down to 0.677). By the rule written before the run (a loss between 0.010 and 0.020 counts as partial dependence), the result depends partly on how the glottal stop is written. The answer to the first reviewer's question is therefore: nearly the same result, not the same result. No noise estimate exists for these differences, so their size relative to chance variation is not known.

Digraphs matter little. The conversion of *ng* and *ny* in all lists and of *gh*, *bh* and *dh* in Muna to single symbols changes 75 forms (Muna 54, Tolaki 20, Sa'dan Toraja 1; *ny* occurs in no form, *gh* in no Wolio form) and leaves the AUC unchanged (−0.0003 with both input sets, +0.003 with the form alone). The two Muna forms with *dh*, *akaradhaa* 'to work' and *idho* 'green', are both coded and were both converted; the earlier version of this paper omitted *dh* from the list, and no loanword was removed from the classifier's data. Word size, on the other hand, is one of the things the classifier uses: removing the length input alone changes nothing, because the vowel count carries the same information, but removing both size inputs costs 0.014 of AUC with both input sets and 0.023 with the form alone. The earlier version's statement that the result does not depend on form length is withdrawn, as is its statement that the profile is not an orthographic artefact: the glottal-mark result above is exactly a dependence on orthography, of modest size.

## Are uncoded forms similar across lists?

The earlier version of this paper reported a clustering analysis and a test with p = 0.569 as evidence that the uncoded forms share no word families across the lists. The test compared a mean over twenty meanings with single draws of mostly coded vocabulary and did not test that hypothesis; the clustering's "optimal" number of clusters was the edge of the range searched. Both are withdrawn and replaced by the permutation test of Section 2.6.

Among the uncoded forms without numerals (411 forms, 440 same-meaning pairs across lists), the mean distance between same-meaning forms is 0.836, against 0.869 when meanings are shuffled (permutation standard deviation 0.006; none of 10,000 permutations reached the observed value). Four pairs are look-alikes at the 0.34 threshold, against 0.52 expected (p = 0.0016). The positive control shows what a shared layer looks like in the same measure: among the coded forms (1,793 pairs) the mean distance is 0.578 against 0.856, with 472 look-alike pairs against 8.5 expected. The excess among uncoded forms is about one eighth of the excess among coded forms on the distance measure and about one twenty-ninth on look-alike pairs. The four pairs are Bugis *ma-kunru* and Wolio *makundu* 'dull', Wolio *tawa* and Tolaki *tawa* 'leaf', Muna *notente* and Wolio *tente* 'to swell', and Bugis *ma-tanəʔ* and Wolio *matamo* 'heavy'; all four involve Wolio, none lies within South Sulawesi, and two begin with *ma-* on both sides. The meanings that pull the statistic down most are quality terms whose uncoded forms begin with *ma-*, *mo-* or *me-* in several lists ('heavy' in all four of its forms, 'dull' in three of four, 'near' in three of three), so part of the small excess may be a shared affix rather than shared roots. Removing any single meaning changes the mean distance by at most 0.003. Pairs inside South Sulawesi (83) show an excess of 0.046 and the other pairs (357) one of 0.030; the difference between the two is about one combined standard deviation and cannot be read. Among the 172 "uncoded, profile" forms there are too few pairs (68) to tell.

What this test does not show is as important as what it shows. Shared vocabulary that ABVD's editors have recognised carries a cognate-set number and is by definition in the coded class, which this test does not examine; a shared layer beyond Proto-Malayo-Polynesian, if there were one, would sit there. Three of the four subgroups represented fall inside the proposed Celebic supergroup, so "across four subgroups" overstates their independence. The paper therefore makes no claim about a shared pre-Austronesian layer, positive or negative, and it does not describe the uncoded forms as independent innovations either: the data show a small excess of similarity among uncoded forms of the same meaning, of a size far below that of the coded vocabulary, and nothing more. More than twenty p-values were computed in this and the preceding sections; only the one for the primary permutation test had its decision rule written before the run.

## Sixteen further lists

An earlier version applied the model to sixteen further ABVD lists from Sulawesi and western Indonesia and reported a geographic pattern. That application used two inputs computed at the level of the list, and the per-list rates it reported depended on them. Without any list-level input, the model ranks the uncoded forms of those sixteen lists against their own ABVD label with a mean AUC of 0.638 (range 0.427 to 0.795; 0.60 or more in ten of the sixteen). The mean score of the Sulawesi lists is higher than that of the western lists (0.308 against 0.229, p = 0.023), but among coded forms alone the difference is not detectable (0.242 against 0.208, p = 0.37), and we make no geographic claim.

# Discussion

*What the profile is.* The measured content of Section 3.5 is a correlated bundle: uncoded forms in these six lists are about one character longer, more often carry a written glottal mark where the source writes one, and more often denote actions; the glottal mark and the action meaning hold at equal length, and the onset-string and nasal inputs do not separate from length. Everything beyond that is interpretation. The reading that the uncoded forms are morphologically complex citation forms is plausible, but it was not tested, and the rival reading that affixed forms are harder to code fits the same numbers. We note that the first reviewer's report contains no morphological reading of this kind; its observation, in a different connection, is that loanwords are commonly adapted to the phonology of the borrowing language (as Blust 2012:556 states for Palauan), so that a regular-looking form is not thereby inherited, and an irregular-looking one is a form worth a closer look rather than an error of the database.

*What the classifier is for.* It is a ranking aid of moderate strength, not a detector of substrate vocabulary. Its AUC of 0.73 means that an uncoded form outranks a coded form about three times in four; its accuracy as a yes/no classifier is close to the trivial rule, and for a list it has not seen it is below the majority answer. The ranking does carry over to unseen lists, with an AUC of 0.70 on average. We see one use for it: ordering the uncoded forms of a list so that a specialist's limited time goes first to the forms that least resemble the coded vocabulary, with the understanding that the model's score encodes length, a written glottal mark and an action meaning and nothing deeper.

*Makasar.* The study adds to the discussion of Makasar's divergence a count that previous counts did not give: on ABVD's coding, Makasar has more uncoded meanings than Bugis and Sa'dan Toraja by 11 to 16 points, and only a small fraction of those uncoded forms resemble the PMP entry or the relatives' forms by a mechanical screen whose ceiling is known. The counts are compatible with the lexical divergence reported by Mills (1975), Sirk (1989) and Bulbeck, Pasqua and Di Lello (2000); they do not prove it, since a spelling screen is not a cognate judgement, and they say nothing about its cause. The agreement between our 39.3 percent and the 38 percent quoted from Blust is an agreement between two counts of the same kind and should not be read as confirmation. What would decide the matter is a specialist's judgement of the 70 uncoded Makasar meanings against their Bugis and Sa'dan Toraja forms; the list is released for that purpose.

*Tolaki.* The word under-documentation is withdrawn. The Tolaki list is well filled; it sits on the lower side of a thinly coded subgroup, and most of its uncoded forms recur within Bungku-Tolaki. Seven of them are coding gaps in the plain sense, and twelve resemble the PMP entry by the screen. None of this bears on origin, for the reason given in Section 3.3.

*Similarity across lists.* Among uncoded forms, same-meaning forms are slightly more alike across lists than different-meaning forms. The excess is small, part of it may be shared affixation, and the coded class, where recognised shared vocabulary lives, was not examined. The paper has no cross-language claim.

*Limitations.* The inputs are written forms, not phonemic transcriptions, and the lists differ in notation; the glottal mark is written routinely in four lists, in four forms of a fifth and not at all in the sixth, and whether that is notation or phonology is not known to us. No form was morphologically segmented, so the morphological reading of the profile is untested. The label is the state of cognate coding in one snapshot of one database; "coded" mixes inherited forms, small local sets and flagged loans, the coding rate differs between lists and subgroups, and one language has two lists that code each other. The properties of the profile are correlated, the intervals treat forms as independent although meanings recur across the six lists, and ten properties were tested without adjustment. Six lists from one island were used. The look-alike screen compares spellings, its 42 Bungku-Tolaki comparison lists represent 13 languages from two contributors, and its sensitivity is a ceiling. Of the many tests reported, one had its decision rule written in advance, on the same day and by the same analyst, without external registration. Form inputs carry information about the source list even though no list-level input is used. And no single form has been assessed by a specialist in these languages.

# Conclusion

Four things can be said. First, the uncoded forms of six Sulawesi ABVD lists can be ranked against the coded forms from their written shape and their meaning, with an AUC of about 0.73, but not reliably classified; the ranking carries over to a list the model has not seen. Second, the properties that distinguish them are a correlated bundle of length, a written glottal mark and an action meaning; the morphological reading of that bundle is a reading. Third, for Makasar and for Tolaki the absence of a cognate-set number means different things, and in neither case does it mean non-Austronesian origin: Makasar's uncoded forms are numerous and mostly unlike their relatives' forms by a mechanical screen; Tolaki's are mostly forms that recur within its subgroup. A cognate-set number, equally, is not a finding of inheritance. Fourth, among uncoded forms the similarity across lists is small, and the test does not reach the class where a shared layer would be found. Three directions remain honest: morphological segmentation before any property is computed; cognate coding at the level of the subgroup, where most of Tolaki's uncoded forms would find their sets; and specialist inspection of the released lists, which is the only step that can turn a profile into etymologies. Full conversion of the written forms to a phonetic transcription (Anderson et al. 2018) would remove the dependence on notation that Section 3.6 measured.
