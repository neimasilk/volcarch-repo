## Abstract

The conventional periodisation of Nusantaran history places its beginning at the emergence of epigraphic and monumental evidence in the 4th century CE. The VOLCARCH hypothesis (Amien & Gunawan, Papers 1–15) argues that this boundary reflects taphonomic loss — volcanic burial, coastal submersion, organic material decay — rather than genuine cultural absence. The present paper advances a complementary line of evidence: **textual archaeology**. We conduct a systematic survey of external textual sources — Greek, Roman, Indian, Chinese, and Arab — that reference Nusantaran commodities, maritime actors, or geographic entities prior to or independent of the 4th-century epigraphic horizon. Across six distinct textual traditions spanning approximately 300 BCE to 400 CE, we identify convergent references to a maritime civilisation in the eastern Indian Ocean that is technologically sophisticated, commercially dominant, and culturally distinctive — yet leaves minimal archaeological trace in its homeland. The pattern of external visibility combined with internal archaeological silence is precisely what the VOLCARCH taphonomic hypothesis predicts. We argue that these external texts constitute a form of distributed archive, preserving records of a Nusantaran world whose physical evidence has been systematically erased by the volcanic and coastal depositional processes documented elsewhere in this series. We further identify a corpus of undertranslated or underutilised primary sources — particularly Chinese dynastic records and Pali Buddhist texts — that represent a significant untapped resource for pre-4th century Nusantaran history.

**Keywords:** Nusantara; pre-4th century; textual archaeology; k'un-lun po; Suvarnabhumi; Chryse; VOLCARCH; Indian Ocean trade; taphonomy; external sources

## 1. Introduction: The Paradox of External Visibility

The conventional narrative of Nusantaran history contains a structural paradox. The region that produced the largest maritime trade network in the pre-modern Indian Ocean world — controlling the flow of cloves, nutmeg, camphor, dammar, and pepper to markets from Rome to China — is archaeologically near-invisible before the 4th century CE. Monumental archaeology begins with the stone inscriptions and candi of the early Hindu-Buddhist kingdoms. Before that horizon, the standard account implies an essentially empty stage.

The VOLCARCH hypothesis addresses the physical dimension of this paradox: volcanic sedimentation buries sites at 3.5–7 mm/yr in Java's volcanic plains, coastal submersion removes littoral settlements, and organic material — the dominant building technology of pre-stone Nusantara — decays completely in tropical conditions. The absence of physical evidence is explained by the systematic destruction of that evidence by environmental processes operating over centuries and millennia.

The present paper addresses a second dimension of the same paradox. If the taphonomic argument is correct, then evidence of pre-4th century Nusantaran civilisation should be detectable not in the homeland — where it has been erased — but in the records of external civilisations that encountered Nusantaran traders, sailors, and commodities. Those external civilisations — Rome, India, China, the Arab world — used stone, clay, and durable materials that preserve well. Their records of Nusantara constitute a distributed archive of a world that cannot speak for itself.

This paper conducts a systematic survey of that distributed archive. We ask: what do external textual sources, from approximately 300 BCE to 400 CE, tell us about Nusantaran maritime activity, cultural complexity, and commercial organisation? And does the picture that emerges from those sources contradict, confirm, or modify the taphonomic interpretation of the archaeological silence?

## 2. Methodology: Textual Archaeology

### 2.1 Definition and Scope

We use "textual archaeology" to mean the systematic analysis of external textual sources for evidence about a society that has left insufficient physical remains for conventional archaeological study. The approach is established in Indian Ocean historiography (Prange 2011; Sheriff 2010) but has not been applied systematically to the specific question of pre-4th century Nusantaran cultural complexity.

Our corpus is bounded by three criteria:

1. **Temporal:** Sources produced or describing conditions before 400 CE, or sources drawing on earlier information
2. **External:** Sources produced outside the Nusantaran archipelago by authors without direct access to the VOLCARCH physical record
3. **Specific:** Sources containing references sufficiently specific to identify Nusantaran commodities, actors, or geographic entities (as opposed to generic "eastern trade" references)

We do not claim that every reference in our corpus is unambiguous. Geographic identification of ancient toponyms is notoriously difficult, and we indicate the scholarly consensus (or contested status) of each identification. Our argument does not depend on any single identification; it rests on the convergence of multiple independent traditions across multiple centuries.

### 2.2 The Distributed Archive Concept

External sources constitute a distributed archive in a specific sense: they were produced independently, in different languages, by authors with different interests and different access to information about Nusantara. When these independent sources converge on consistent descriptions — of commodities, of maritime capability, of geographic location — the convergence provides evidence that cannot be explained by textual transmission alone. Multiple independent witnesses to the same phenomenon represent stronger evidence than a single internally consistent account.

This is the same logic that underlies the cross-Austronesian comparison methodology used in Papers 5 and 15 of this series: when independent cultures share structural features, the sharing is evidence of common origin or common environmental forcing that cannot be explained by coincidence or borrowing.

## 3. Greek and Roman Sources (~300 BCE – 200 CE)

### 3.1 Eratosthenes and the Chryse Tradition (~276–195 BCE)

The earliest Greek reference to a "Golden Land" in the far east of the Indian Ocean is attributed to Eratosthenes of Cyrene (ca. 276–195 BCE), the librarian of Alexandria and the first person to calculate the Earth's circumference. In his geographical writings (preserved only in fragments through Strabo), Eratosthenes described a *Chryse* ("Gold") island or promontory at the eastern extremity of the known world.

The significance for our purposes is chronological. Eratosthenes was writing approximately six centuries before the conventional start of Nusantaran history. The knowledge he encoded — derived from Hellenistic merchant networks connected to the Ptolemaic Egyptian trade system — implies that Nusantara was already sufficiently prominent in Indian Ocean commerce by the 3rd century BCE to have reached the attention of the Mediterranean world's leading geographer.

### 3.2 Periplus of the Erythraean Sea (~40–55 CE)

The *Periplus Maris Erythraei*, an anonymous Greek-language merchant's guide written in the mid-1st century CE, contains the most detailed early Western account of Indian Ocean trade. The Periplus describes trade routes, commodities, and ports from Egypt to the eastern limit of its author's knowledge. At the far eastern end, it mentions an island *Chryse* "at the very end of the inhabitable world towards the east, lying directly under the rising sun itself." The Chryse reference is accompanied by a description of tortoiseshell as its primary export — a commodity consistent with Southeast Asian archipelago environments.

The Periplus represents first-hand merchant knowledge: the author explicitly states that the information comes from direct trading experience. The eastern Indian Ocean trading network it describes was operational before its composition, and the consistency of the Chryse reference with Eratosthenes' earlier account suggests a continuous tradition of information about the Nusantaran archipelago reaching the Mediterranean world across at least two centuries before the Periplus was written.

### 3.3 Ptolemy's Geographia (~150 CE)

Claudius Ptolemy's *Geographia* (ca. 150 CE) contains the most geographically specific classical Western account of the eastern Indian Ocean. Ptolemy's map includes:

- **Aurea Chersonesus** (*Chrysē Chersonēsos*): the "Golden Peninsula," identified by modern scholars with the Malay Peninsula
- **Sinda** and **Sabarana**: place names on the Malay/Sumatran coast
- **Iabadiu** (also spelled *Iabadiou*): identified by several scholars as Java (*Yavadvipa*)

The Iabadiu identification is particularly significant. Ptolemy describes it as an island of extraordinary fertility producing gold in abundance, with a city at its western end called *Argyrē* ("Silver city"). The identification with Java remains debated, but the geographic position — southeast of the Golden Peninsula, near the equator — is consistent.

Critically, Ptolemy's information derived from **Marinus of Tyre** (ca. 100 CE), who in turn cited a sailor named **Alexander** who had personally visited the Aurea Chersonesus region. This transmission chain suggests that direct maritime contact between Mediterranean networks and Nusantara was established by at least the late 1st century CE — and probably considerably earlier, given that Marinus described the information as already existing.

### 3.4 Roman Archaeological Evidence: The Dammar Connection

The strongest physical corroboration of the Greek-Roman textual tradition comes not from texts but from chemistry. Analysis of embalming vessels from a 26th Dynasty (664–525 BCE) workshop at Saqqara, Egypt, has identified **dammar resin** — sourced from *Shorea* species trees found specifically in Indonesia and Southeast Asia — and **elemi resin** from *Canarium* species of tropical Asia and Africa (Rageot et al. 2023, *Nature*). The dating of this evidence (664–525 BCE) predates even Eratosthenes, pushing direct commodity exchange between Egypt and Nusantara to the first millennium BCE.

This chemical evidence does not require that Egyptian merchants visited Nusantara directly. Dammar and elemi could have reached Egypt through intermediary chains (Indian, Arab, East African traders). But it establishes that Nusantaran forest products were integrated into Mediterranean economic systems before any written record of that integration.

The word *camphor* itself encodes this history. Its etymology traces through Old French, Medieval Latin, Arabic (*kāfūr*), and Sanskrit (*karpūra*) back to Old Malay *kapur barus* — "the chalk of Barus," the ancient Sumatran camphor port. The linguistic transmission chain from Malay through Sanskrit to Arabic to Latin to English preserves, in a single word, the archaeology of a trade network that is otherwise largely invisible.

## 4. Indian Sources (~500 BCE – 400 CE)

### 4.1 The Pali Buddhist Tradition: Suvarnabhumi

The Pali Buddhist textual tradition contains the earliest and most extensive Indian references to Nusantara. Three texts are of particular relevance:

**Jataka Tales (~4th century BCE compilation)**
The Jataka collection of Buddhist birth stories contains multiple narratives involving merchant voyages to *Suvarnabhumi* — "the Land of Gold." The *Supparaka Jataka*, *Baveru Jataka*, and *Sankha Jataka* all describe sea voyages to wealthy eastern lands. While the Jataka tales are literary narratives, not geographical treatises, their consistent reference to a prosperous eastern maritime destination reflects real knowledge of Indian Ocean trade routes current at the time of their composition.

**Milinda Panha ("The Questions of King Milinda," ~100 BCE – 200 CE)**
This Pali philosophical dialogue between the Greek king Menander and the Buddhist monk Nagasena contains a passage in which Nagasena uses the example of a merchant who "has sailed to Suvarnabhumi" to illustrate a philosophical point. The casual deployment of the Suvarnabhumi voyage as a familiar example suggests that such voyages were a common commercial reality known to the dialogue's intended audience by at least the 1st century BCE.

**Mahavamsa (~5th century CE, describing events from ~543 BCE)**
The Sri Lankan chronicle describes Prince Vijaya's legendary voyage and subsequent events, with references to *Suvarnabhumi* as a destination for merchants and adventurers. While the Mahavamsa's historical reliability for its early sections is contested, its consistent location of Suvarnabhumi in the eastern Indian Ocean, reachable by sea from Sri Lanka, is geographically consistent with Nusantara.

The *Suvarnabhumi* identification remains scholarly debated — candidates include Burma, Thailand, and the Malay/Indonesian archipelago. However, later inscriptional evidence from Nusantara itself clarifies the picture: a 1286 CE inscription from Padangroco explicitly identifies *Suvarnabhumi* with Sumatra (Tanjungemas), and the *Nagarakretagama* (1336 CE) uses *Suvarnabhumi* to refer to Sumatra. The later Nusantaran sources corroborate the identification of *Suvarnabhumi* with Sumatra, suggesting that the Pali references encode genuine knowledge of the same region.

### 4.2 Sanskrit Epics: Yavadvipa in the Ramayana

The *Ramayana* of Valmiki (date debated: 5th–4th century BCE composition with later redactions) contains a passage in the *Kishkindha Kanda* (4.40.30) instructing the monkey search party to seek Sita on the island of *Yavadvipa* — "Island of Barley/Millet" — described as rich in gold and silver and home to seven kingdoms. While the passage is part of an epic narrative, the name *Yavadvipa* is almost universally identified by scholars with Java (*Java* being the Sanskrit *Yava*, barley/millet; *dvipa*, island).

The Ramayana reference places knowledge of Java in what is at minimum the early centuries BCE, and possibly much earlier if the textual tradition accurately preserves pre-redaction geography. The description of seven kingdoms on Yavadvipa — whatever its mythological character — implies a knowledge of political complexity in the island that is inconsistent with an uninhabited or culturally simple landscape.

### 4.3 Kautilya's Arthashastra (~300 BCE – 200 CE)

The *Arthashastra*, the classical Indian treatise on statecraft attributed to Kautilya (Chanakya), contains references to trade goods from *dvipantara* — "the other islands" — including specific aromatic and forest products consistent with Nusantaran origins. The *Arthashastra*'s treatment of maritime trade as a regulated economic activity implies that such trade was sufficiently established by the Maurya period (4th–3rd century BCE) to require state regulation.

## 5. Chinese Sources (~3rd Century BCE – 4th Century CE)

### 5.1 Han Dynasty Records and the Nanhai Trade

Chinese knowledge of the southern seas (*Nanhai*) predates the Han dynasty, but systematic documentation begins with Han-era geographical records. The *Hanshu* (Book of Han, completed ca. 111 CE but describing 2nd–1st century BCE conditions) describes maritime routes from southern Chinese ports that reach "the land of Huang-zhi" (identified with Kancipuram in South India) via islands in the southern sea. The route description implies passage through or near the Nusantaran archipelago.

More significantly, the *Hanshu* records the products brought back by Han maritime missions: pearls, glass, exotic gemstones, and aromatic woods — the last consistent with Nusantaran forest products.

### 5.2 Wan Chen's *Nanzhou Yiwu Zhi* (~264 CE) — The Most Important Underutilised Source

The most detailed early Chinese account of Nusantaran maritime technology is Wan Chen's *Nanzhou Yiwu Zhi* (南州異物志, "Record of Strange Things from the Southern Regions"), written during the Three Kingdoms period (ca. 264 CE). This text contains the earliest detailed description of the *k'un-lun po* (崑崙舶) — the large sailing vessels operated by Austronesian traders from Southeast Asia.

Wan Chen's description is remarkably specific:

> *"The people of foreign parts call ships po. The large ones are more than fifty metres in length and stand out of the water four to five metres. A single such vessel carries between six hundred and seven hundred persons, with cargo of 10,000 hu."*

The *k'un-lun po* are described as multi-masted vessels with woven bamboo sails, capable of sailing against the wind — a reference to the Austronesian crab-claw sail technology. This is the earliest explicit description of what modern naval archaeology identifies as the Southeast Asian outrigger and lashed-lug construction tradition.

The significance of the *Nanzhou Yiwu Zhi* for VOLCARCH cannot be overstated. Wan Chen was writing in 264 CE — approximately one century before the first Sanskrit inscriptions in Nusantara. He is describing not a beginning but an established, sophisticated maritime technology that had been operational for an unknown prior period. The k'un-lun po he describes are clearly the product of a long tradition of shipbuilding refinement, not a newly emerged capability.

### 5.3 Buddhist Pilgrim Records: Faxian (399–414 CE)

The monk Faxian's *Foguoji* (佛國記, "Record of Buddhist Kingdoms") describes his return voyage from India to China via a stop on an island he calls *Ye-po-ti* (耶婆提). The identification of Ye-po-ti with Java (*Yavadvipa*) is accepted by most scholars. Faxian's description of Ye-po-ti as a place where Brahmanical religion flourishes and Buddhism is little practiced confirms the Hinduization of Java by the early 5th century — but more significantly, it implies that Faxian's Chinese Buddhist readers already knew what Ye-po-ti was. The place name required no explanation in his text.

This "no explanation needed" inference is methodologically important. When a text references a location without glossing it, the author assumes the audience's familiarity. Faxian's casual reference to Ye-po-ti implies that Java was already part of the standard geographical knowledge of educated Chinese monks by the early 5th century — a status requiring decades or centuries of prior commercial and cultural contact to establish.

### 5.4 The k'un-lun po in Tang Dynasty Records

Later Tang dynasty records (particularly Hui-Lin's *Yiqiejing Yinyi*, ca. 817 CE) contain retrospective descriptions of the k'un-lun po that confirm the Wan Chen account and provide additional detail about the vessels' construction. The consistency between the 3rd-century and 9th-century accounts suggests a continuous tradition of Nusantaran maritime technology, not a sudden emergence.

## 6. The Chemical Archive: Commodity Evidence Independent of Texts

### 6.1 Dammar and Elemi at Saqqara (664–525 BCE)

As discussed in Section 3.4, chemical analysis of embalming vessels from Saqqara has identified dammar and elemi resins of Southeast Asian/Indonesian origin in contexts dating to 664–525 BCE. This represents the earliest physical evidence of Nusantaran commodity integration into Mediterranean economic systems — predating all textual evidence by two to three centuries.

### 6.2 The Austronesian Spice Network (~1500–600 BCE)

Archaeological and archaeobotanical evidence from Sri Lanka and South India documents the introduction of Austronesian crops — coconuts, bananas, sugarcane, sandalwood — and sailing technologies (outrigger boats) by approximately 1500–600 BCE. This evidence implies organised, recurring maritime contact between Nusantara and South Asia beginning in the Bronze Age — approximately two millennia before the conventional start of Nusantaran history.

### 6.3 Clove and Nutmeg at Mediterranean Sites

Cloves (*Syzygium aromaticum*), native exclusively to the North Maluku (Maluku Utara) islands of modern Indonesia, have been identified at archaeological sites in Syria dating to approximately 1700 BCE, and in a cooking context at the ancient site of Terqa on the Euphrates. If confirmed, this represents the earliest known long-distance transport of an exclusively Nusantaran product — approximately 2,000 years before the conventional start of Nusantaran history.

## 7. The Arab Tradition: Barus/Fansur as Case Study

### 7.1 The Medieval Arabic Evidence

The Arabic geographical and pharmacological tradition contains the most extensive pre-modern documentation of Nusantaran commodities, particularly camphor. The Arab geographical tradition consistently identifies the finest camphor in the world as coming from *Fansur* (later *Barus*) on the western coast of Sumatra. Sources include:

- **Al-Jahiz** (*Kitab al-Hayawan*, ~847 CE): camphor as a primary aromatic
- **Ibn Khurdadhbeh** (*Kitab al-Masalik wa-l-Mamalik*, ~885 CE): Fansur as source of the best camphor
- **Al-Masudi** (*Muruj al-Dhahab*, ~943 CE): detailed description of Sumatra and Java
- **Al-Nuwayri** (*Nihayat al-Arab*, ~1333 CE): Fansur as "Land of Gold," camphor of unmatched quality

### 7.2 The Etymology as Fossil Trade Route

The word *camphor* encodes the history of its own trade. The etymological chain — Malay *kapur barus* → Sanskrit *karpūra* → Arabic *kāfūr* → Medieval Latin *camfora* → Old French *camphre* → English *camphor* — represents the successive linguistic relabelling of a commodity as it moved through different trading hands. Each link in the chain implies not merely knowledge of the commodity but active commercial engagement with it.

The presence of Sanskrit *karpūra* as an intermediate form implies that the camphor trade was routed through South Asian commercial networks before reaching the Arab world — consistent with the Indian Ocean trade archaeology discussed in Section 6. The fact that Sanskrit borrowed the Malay term rather than coining its own implies that Malay speakers were the original suppliers within the Sanskrit-sphere commercial network.

## 8. Synthesis: The Picture from Outside

### 8.1 A Convergent Portrait

Across six independent textual traditions — Greek, Roman, Indian (Pali and Sanskrit), Chinese, chemical/archaeobotanical, and Arab — a convergent portrait emerges:

**Table 1: Summary of External Evidence for Pre-4th Century Nusantaran Activity**

| Source Tradition | Earliest Reference | Type of Evidence | Nusantaran Activity Implied |
|---|---|---|---|
| Greek (Eratosthenes) | ~250 BCE | Geographic knowledge | Commercial prominence |
| Chemical (Saqqara) | 664–525 BCE | Physical residue | Commodity export |
| Indian (Pali Jataka) | ~4th c. BCE | Literary reference | Maritime trade destination |
| Indian (Ramayana) | ~4th–2nd c. BCE | Epic geography | Political complexity (7 kingdoms) |
| Chemical (spice network) | ~1500–600 BCE | Archaeobotanical | Maritime technology export |
| Chinese (Hanshu) | ~2nd c. BCE | Dynastic record | Trade route integration |
| Chinese (Wan Chen) | 264 CE | Descriptive text | Sophisticated shipbuilding |
| Roman (Periplus) | ~50 CE | Merchant guide | Commercial geography |
| Roman (Ptolemy) | ~150 CE | Geographic treatise | Political geography |

The earliest evidence in this table (chemical, ~1500–600 BCE) predates the conventional start of Nusantaran history by approximately 1,500–2,000 years. Even the textual evidence, beginning with Eratosthenes (~250 BCE), predates the conventional start by approximately 650 years.

### 8.2 The Sophistication Implied

What does the convergent portrait tell us about pre-4th century Nusantaran culture? Several inferences follow from the evidence:

**Maritime technology:** The k'un-lun po described by Wan Chen in 264 CE are sophisticated multi-masted vessels capable of trans-oceanic voyaging and carrying 600–700 passengers with cargo of 10,000 hu. This technology did not emerge in 264 CE; Wan Chen is describing an established tradition. The Austronesian outrigger and crab-claw sail technology documented archaeologically in Sri Lanka and South India from ~1500 BCE provides the baseline from which the k'un-lun po tradition developed over the intervening millennia.

**Commercial organisation:** The consistent appearance of Nusantaran commodities — camphor, dammar, elemi, cloves, nutmeg — in Mediterranean, South Asian, and Chinese commercial contexts implies organised, repeating, large-scale production and export. Ad hoc or occasional exchange does not generate the pattern of consistent commodity integration documented across these traditions. This implies settled populations, specialised production, and the social organisation necessary to sustain long-distance trade — the characteristics of what we would conventionally call "complex society."

**Geographic knowledge:** The consistent geographic positioning of Nusantara in external sources — southeast of India, east of the "Golden Peninsula," at the eastern edge of the known world — implies that external traders and geographers had sufficient repeated contact with Nusantaran actors to develop accurate geographic knowledge. This is not the pattern expected from incidental or accidental contact.

### 8.3 The VOLCARCH Interpretation

The picture from outside is precisely what the VOLCARCH taphonomic hypothesis predicts. A maritime civilisation whose primary settlement sites are coastal (vulnerable to sea-level rise and coastal erosion), whose primary building technology is organic (vulnerable to tropical decay), and whose homeland is subject to periodic volcanic burial (documented at 3.5–7 mm/yr in Java's volcanic plains) would be expected to leave minimal physical trace in its homeland while being well-documented by the durable-material civilisations it traded with.

The external textual evidence does not prove that Java or Sumatra had complex polities before 400 CE. What it proves is that Nusantaran actors — traders, sailors, commodity producers — were integrated into the major civilisational networks of the ancient world from at least the mid-first millennium BCE, and probably from the second millennium BCE. That level of economic and maritime integration implies a supporting social infrastructure that the domestic archaeological record — compromised by taphonomic loss — has not yet revealed.

## 9. The Untapped Corpus: Directions for Future Research

### 9.1 Underutilised Chinese Sources

The most significant untapped resource for pre-4th century Nusantaran history is the corpus of early Chinese dynastic records and traveller accounts. Several texts have received insufficient attention in Nusantaran historiography:

**Wan Chen's *Nanzhou Yiwu Zhi* (264 CE):** Only partially translated into Western languages. A complete critical edition with geographic annotation focused on Nusantaran references would be a significant contribution.

**Kang Tai's *Funan Tuqi* (~260 CE):** Kang Tai visited the kingdom of Funan (Mekong Delta) and wrote an account of the region that includes references to maritime networks extending to the Indonesian archipelago. Largely untranslated.

**Zhang Hua's *Bowuzhi* (~290 CE):** Contains descriptions of southern sea products and peoples. Partially relevant to Nusantaran history.

**Faxian's *Foguoji* (414 CE):** Fully translated but the Ye-po-ti passage has not been analysed in the context of VOLCARCH-type taphonomic arguments.

### 9.2 The Sanskrit Inscription Gap

The DHARMA corpus of 268 Nusantaran inscriptions begins with the Yupa inscriptions of Kutai (~400 CE). The gap between the Sanskrit-knowledge implied by these first inscriptions — which demonstrate sophisticated command of Sanskrit metrical and theological conventions — and the supposed date of first Sanskrit contact is unexplained by the conventional chronology. The earliest Nusantaran inscriptions are not tentative first attempts at Sanskrit literacy; they are accomplished compositions. This implies a prior period of Sanskrit learning that is not represented in the physical record — another potential taphonomic absence.

### 9.3 Close Reading of Primary Chinese Texts: A Methodological Opportunity

A largely unexplored methodological opportunity lies in the close reading of primary Chinese texts in their original Classical Chinese (文言文), cross-referenced against existing Western translations. Existing Western translations of key sources — notably Legge (1886) on Faxian and Wheatley (1961) on Wan Chen — were produced for general historical purposes and were not oriented toward the specific questions of Nusantaran geographic identification or taphonomic interpretation.

A close re-reading of these texts with VOLCARCH questions foregrounded may yield disambiguating detail that existing translations have not rendered. Specific candidates for this analysis include:

**Faxian's *Foguoji* — Ye-po-ti passage:**
Faxian records his sailing time from Sri Lanka to Ye-po-ti (耶婆提) and back to China, including wind direction and sea conditions. These navigational details, combined with monsoon pattern analysis, can be used to constrain the geographic identification of Ye-po-ti independent of linguistic arguments. A sailing-time + wind-direction calculation — what might be called a *navigational triangulation* — could contribute meaningfully to the Ye-po-ti = Java vs. Sumatra debate. This type of analysis has been applied to other ancient voyage accounts (e.g., the *Periplus*) but not systematically to Faxian's Southeast Asian passages.

**Wan Chen's *Nanzhou Yiwu Zhi* — k'un-lun po passage:**
Wan Chen's description of the k'un-lun po contains specific technical detail about construction and rigging. The original Chinese text may contain terminology that existing translations have rendered generically, but which, when read against the archaeologically documented Austronesian shipbuilding tradition (sewn-plank, lashed-lug, outrigger construction), could identify specific regional variants or construction traditions. This could narrow the identification of the k'un-lun po's home ports within the Nusantaran archipelago.

**Methodological framing:**
Contributions from this type of close reading should be framed conservatively: *"A close reading of the original text suggests detail not fully rendered in existing translations..."* or *"The Chinese original contains navigational specifics that constrain the geographic identification as follows..."* This framing is appropriate because re-readings of classical texts, even when yielding new nuance, require peer scrutiny from sinologists before constituting standalone scholarly claims. Within the VOLCARCH framework, however, such readings function as **corroborating nuance** rather than primary claims — a methodologically appropriate role.

**Priority texts for close reading (accessible via zh.wikisource.org and ctext.org):**
1. Faxian, *Foguoji* 佛國記 — Ye-po-ti passage (chapters on return voyage)
2. Wan Chen, *Nanzhou Yiwu Zhi* 南州異物志 — k'un-lun po passage
3. Kang Tai, *Funan Tuqi* 扶南土俗 — fragments preserved in *Taiping Yulan* 太平御覽
4. *Liang Shu* 梁書, Chapters on "Island Barbarians" (海南諸國傳) — earliest formal dynastic account of Nusantaran polities

### 9.4 Archaeobotanical Evidence from Port Sites

The archaeobotanical evidence for Nusantaran commodities in external contexts (Saqqara dammar; Mediterranean cloves; South Indian Austronesian crops) has a physical correlate: the port sites through which these commodities transited. Survey of known early port sites in Sri Lanka, South India, and East Africa for Nusantaran commodity residues using the gas chromatography methods pioneered by Rageot et al. (2023) represents a high-value research direction.

## 10. Discussion

### 10.1 The Argument from Convergence

The evidentiary argument of this paper is an argument from convergence. No single source in our corpus is unambiguous. Geographic identifications are debated; literary texts conflate myth and geography; chemical evidence requires chain-of-custody reconstruction. But the convergence of independent traditions — Greek, Indian, Chinese, chemical — across a time span of nearly two millennia, consistently pointing to a maritime civilisation in the eastern Indian Ocean with specific commodity outputs and sophisticated maritime technology, cannot be explained by coincidence or textual transmission.

The convergence argument mirrors the logic of the taphonomic argument elsewhere in the VOLCARCH series. Just as the convergence of geological evidence (sedimentation rates), archaeological evidence (buried candi), and ritual evidence (slametan intervals, nyadran oral tradition) supports the taphonomic hypothesis, the convergence of external textual evidence supports the inference of pre-4th century cultural complexity.

### 10.2 What This Paper Does Not Claim

This paper does not claim to have discovered a Nusantaran civilisation comparable to Egypt or Mesopotamia. It does not claim that the external sources prove the existence of specific polities, monuments, or institutions. It claims only that the external evidence is inconsistent with the interpretation of the domestic archaeological silence as evidence of cultural absence — and that the most parsimonious interpretation of all available evidence (domestic archaeological silence + external textual visibility) is taphonomic loss, not cultural vacancy.

### 10.3 The Burden of Proof

The conventional argument from silence — "no evidence of civilisation before 400 CE, therefore no civilisation before 400 CE" — assigns the burden of proof to those who would argue for earlier complexity. After this paper, that assignment should be questioned. The external evidence reviewed here establishes that Nusantaran actors were commercially integrated into major civilisational networks from at least the mid-first millennium BCE. The question is no longer whether complex social organisation existed in Nusantara before 400 CE, but why that organisation has left so little domestic physical trace. The VOLCARCH hypothesis provides the answer.

## 11. Conclusion

Six independent textual and chemical traditions — Greek, Roman, Indian, Chinese, archaeobotanical, and Arab — converge on a consistent portrait of pre-4th century Nusantara as a maritime civilisation of substantial commercial and technological sophistication. The earliest evidence (Austronesian crops and technology in South India, ~1500–600 BCE; dammar resin in Egyptian embalming workshops, 664–525 BCE) predates the conventional start of Nusantaran history by more than a millennium. The textual evidence, beginning with Eratosthenes (~250 BCE) and running through the k'un-lun po accounts of Wan Chen (264 CE), documents commercial integration, maritime capability, and geographic prominence across the entire period for which external documentation is available.

This external visibility is precisely what the VOLCARCH taphonomic hypothesis predicts. The civilisation that produced the k'un-lun po, exported camphor to Rome and dammar to Egypt, and appeared in the geographical knowledge of Eratosthenes, Ptolemy, and the Jataka compilers was a real historical entity. Its absence from the domestic archaeological record reflects not its non-existence but the systematic destruction of its physical remains by the volcanic and coastal depositional processes that are the subject of this research series.

The living culture records, in ritual, what the volcanic soil has buried. The external archive records, in durable writing, what the organic homeland could not preserve. Together, they point toward a pre-4th century Nusantara that is invisible only to those who look exclusively at the physical record of the homeland.

## Appendix B: The Camphor Etymology as Trade Fossil

The word *camphor* in English is a linguistic fossil of the Barus camphor trade. The complete chain:

**Malay** *kapur barus* ("chalk/lime of Barus") →
**Sanskrit** *karpūra* (borrowed from Malay, not a Sanskrit root) →
**Arabic** *kāfūr* (from Sanskrit) →
**Medieval Latin** *camfora* (from Arabic, via Crusader-period trade) →
**Old French** *camphre* →
**English** *camphor*

The direction of borrowing — Malay → Sanskrit, not Sanskrit → Malay — is linguistically significant. Sanskrit, the prestige language of the Indic trading sphere, borrowed from Malay because Malay speakers were the original suppliers. This single etymological fact encodes the commercial primacy of Nusantaran traders in the regional camphor market at the time when Sanskrit commercial vocabulary was being established — a period contemporary with the Jataka tales and Milinda Panha references to *Suvarnabhumi*.
