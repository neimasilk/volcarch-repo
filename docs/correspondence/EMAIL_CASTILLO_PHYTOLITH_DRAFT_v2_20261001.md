**Subject:** Microbotanical evidence from deep sediments in volcanic Java — is a small test feasible?

Dear Dr Castillo,

I am a computational researcher at Universitas Bhinneka Nusantara in Malang, Indonesia, and I am writing
about a question that I now think only field and laboratory evidence can settle. I would value your view
on whether a small test is feasible.

The question is why the archaeological record of Java's volcanic interior is so thin before about
400 CE, compared with the north coast (the Buni-phase burials at Batujaya, for example). Four
explanations compete: deep volcanic and fluvial burial, the monument-centred history of survey, a
dispersed and largely organic way of life that leaves faint traces, and a genuinely sparse population.
Over the past year and a half I have tested what can be tested from existing data. My honest conclusion
is that desk evidence cannot separate these four. A recent audit of my own work withdrew one result I
had read as evidence for burial, so I am not writing to defend a thesis.

What could separate them is direct evidence of plant use in deep, dated sediments within the volcanic
zone: buried soils exposed in sand-quarry faces on the Merapi or Kelud ring plains, or stored matrix from
excavated sites. In your view, would a phytolith and starch analysis of such sediments be informative,
and is it feasible? I can contribute compilations of sites and burial depths, candidate quarry locations,
and analysis code. The design and any costs would be for us to discuss; this is not a request for
funding.

I would be glad to send a two-page summary first, so that you can judge it before committing any time.

With kind regards,

Mukhlis Amien
Universitas Bhinneka Nusantara, Malang, Indonesia · amien@ubhinus.ac.id
