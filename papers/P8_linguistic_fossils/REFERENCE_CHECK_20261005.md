## Part C - factual look-ups

### C1. Sulawesi "microgroups" in Blust, *The Austronesian Languages*
Source: the open-access 2013 revised edition (A-PL 008), full PDF fetched from https://zorc.net/rdzorc/Blust-RobertA/Blust-2013=Austronesian_Languages.pdf (title page prints http://hdl.handle.net/1885/10191). The 2009 print edition text was not seen, so whether it has identical wording is unverified; the 2013 front matter says the revision has "typographical corrections, an improved index, and various minor content changes".

- Section 2.4.6 "The languages of Sulawesi", printed p. 82 (PDF page 124): "Eleven linguistic 'microgroups' are recognised in Sulawesi: 1. Sangiric ... 2. Minahasan ... 3. Gorontalic ... 4. Tomini-Tolitoli ... 5. Saluan ... 6. Kaili-Pamona ... 7. Bungku-Tolaki ... 8. Muna-Buton ... 9. Wotu-Wolio ... 10. Tukang Besi ... and 11. South Sulawesi ...". It adds that "there is some evidence for a 'Celebic supergroup' that contains groups 4-10, and excludes the South Sulawesi languages (Mead 2003b)", and that the first two groups "are primary branches of the Philippine subgroup, while Gorontalic is part of Greater Central Philippines".
- Section 4.1.6 "Sulawesi", printed p. 193 (PDF page 235): "Sneddon (1993) divided the languages of Sulawesi into ten microgroups: 1) Sangiric, 2) Minahasan, 3) Gorontalo-Mongondow, 4) Tomini, 5) Saluan, 6) Banggai, 7) Kaili-Pamona, 8) Bungku-Tolaki, 9) Muna-Buton, and 10) South Sulawesi. Mead (2003a,b) recognises an additional Sulawesian microgroup, Wotu-Wolio ...", and has shown Banggai belongs in Saluan.
- So the reviewer is right that the book lists eleven (section 2.4.6); the book also reports Sneddon's ten (section 4.1.6). Mead 2003 itself (p. 115) says researchers have parcelled Sulawesi languages "from four to eleven subgroups" and recognises ten.
- Terminology caution for the manuscript sentence "at least ten primary subgroups \citep{blust2009}": Blust says "microgroups" and treats Sangiric and Minahasan as primary branches of the Philippine subgroup (not primary Austronesian subgroups), so "primary subgroups" is not Blust's wording; "eleven microgroups (Blust 2013: 82)" would be the faithful statement.
