## Abstract (~200 words) [HUMAN VOICE]

*[To be written by Pak Amien after full draft. Key elements to include:]*

Denys Lombard described Java as a *carrefour* — a crossroads shaped by three cultural currents: Western modernisation, Islamic-Chinese commerce, and the heritage of the concentric Hindu-Buddhist kingdoms. His metaphor is geological: layers deposited on a landscape. But every landscape has bedrock. This paper asks what lies beneath Lombard's deepest visible layer.

The standard answer, inherited from colonial scholarship and reproduced in Indonesian textbooks, is that pre-Hindu Java was "prehistoric" — before writing, before civilisation, before history. We argue that this answer confuses visibility with existence. Two intersecting formation processes — volcanic sedimentation burying archaeological deposits at measured rates of 2 to 6 millimetres per year, and a historiographic tradition that equates "history" with "inscription" — have rendered the pre-Indic stratum structurally invisible.

We do not claim to have found this layer. We argue that its invisibility has identifiable causes, and that traces of it remain legible in language (Austronesian agricultural vocabulary that Sanskrit never replaced), in ritual (mortuary sequences calibrated to decomposition in volcanic soil), and in spatial practice (temple siting that encodes volcanic hazard awareness). The implications for Indonesian heritage policy and decolonial historiography are discussed.

## 1 Introduction: Lombard's Missing Layer (~1,500 words) [HUMAN VOICE]

### Opening Strategy

*[BKI articles open narratively, not with literature review. Two options:]*

**Option A — The Farmer's Plough (narrative hook, following Griffiths 2024 model):**

In 1966, a farmer ploughing his rice field near Kalasan in central Java struck stone. Excavation revealed a complete Hindu temple complex — Candi Sambisari, built around 835 CE — buried six and a half metres below the modern surface. Nobody had known it was there. A thousand years of volcanic sedimentation, deposited layer by imperceptible layer, had erased the temple from the landscape and from memory.

Sambisari is not exceptional. Candi Kedulan, discovered during road construction in 1993, lay seven metres below ground, sealed beneath fourteen distinct layers of fluvio-volcanic sediment. At the measured sedimentation rates of volcanic Java, anything deposited before the fourth century of the common era now rests at depths that surface archaeology cannot reach. The question this paper asks is not whether earlier deposits exist — they almost certainly do — but why their absence has been mistaken for evidence that the landscape was empty.

### The Standard Answer

The standard answer, inherited from colonial scholarship (Coedes 1968) and reproduced in Indonesian history textbooks (Bloembergen and Eickhoff 2020), is that Java before the fourth century of the common era was essentially "prehistoric" — populated by small-scale societies without writing, without complex political organisation, without the markers that historians recognise as "civilisation." This periodisation places the "beginning" of Javanese history at the moment of first contact with Indian cultural models.

### The Argument of This Paper

This paper argues that the standard answer confuses visibility with existence. The absence of pre-fourth century evidence in volcanic Java is not evidence of pre-fourth century absence. It is the predictable product of two intersecting formation processes:

1. **Taphonomic:** Volcanic sedimentation buries archaeological deposits at rates of 2 to 6 millimetres per year, placing pre-Hindu material beyond the reach of surface survey within centuries.

2. **Historiographic:** The equation of "history" with "inscription" means that the pre-literate period is defined by absence — and since literacy arrived with Indianisation, the pre-Indic period is historiographically invisible by definition.

These two processes are not independent. The colonial and postcolonial periodisation of Indonesian history was constructed from evidence that survived a taphonomic filter. The filter is not random — it systematically removes organic material culture, low-lying settlements, and non-monumental architecture while preserving stone temples, metal inscriptions, and cave deposits. The "history" that emerged from this filtered record is the history of what survived, not the history of what happened.

### What This Paper Does Not Claim

We do not claim to have found the missing layer. We do not claim that pre-Hindu Java was a "lost civilisation" of any particular sophistication. We claim something more modest and more defensible: that the absence of evidence has been mistaken for evidence of absence, and that this mistake has structural causes that can be identified and, in principle, corrected.

*[Footnote here: acknowledge the slogan "absence of evidence is not evidence of absence" and note that in this case the claim is stronger — the absence is PREDICTED by known geological processes.]*

## 2 The Indianisation Debate Revisited (~1,500 words)

### 2.1 The Trajectory

*[Trace the debate: Coedes, van Leur, Wolters, current models. Each gets a paragraph.]*

**Coedes (1968):** Indianisation as cultural diffusion. The "Greater India" model. Southeast Asian societies as passive recipients of Indian civilisation.

**Van Leur (1955):** Critique from within. Indian influence was "a thin, easily flaking glaze" on indigenous cultures. Local traders, not Indian colonisers, drove the contact. But van Leur still worked within the framework of Indian influence as the starting point of historical analysis.

**Wolters (1982, revised 1999):** "Localisation." Southeast Asian societies did not passively receive Indian culture. They actively selected, adapted, and transformed Indian elements to serve local purposes. The "man of prowess" used Hindu devotional cults to augment existing charismatic authority. The genius was local, not imported.

**Current models (Manguin 2004; Kulke 1990):** "Convergence." Neither diffusion nor localisation fully captures the complexity. Multiple agents, multiple directions, multiple timescales.

### 2.2 The Question Nobody Asks

Wolters' localisation is now the dominant framework. But it contains an unexamined assumption: it assumes the local is known. If Indianisation was localisation — if Southeast Asian societies adapted Indian models to serve local cultural needs — then what were those local needs? What was the cultural matrix into which Indian elements were absorbed?

Wolters described this matrix in terms of "men of prowess" and "soul stuff," but these are abstractions derived from the post-Indianisation record. The pre-Indianisation matrix is, by definition, invisible in the written record, because writing arrived with Indianisation.

The silence about the receiving culture is itself a formation process — what Schiffer (1987) would call an N-transform: a natural process that systematically removes information from the analytical frame. In this case, the information lost is the entire pre-literate cultural context of Indianisation.

### 2.3 Linguistic Evidence for the Receiving Culture

*[Light touch — 1-2 paragraphs. Supporting evidence, not main contribution. NO experiment labels.]*

Analysis of Old Javanese vocabulary, drawing on the DHARMA inscription corpus and Austronesian lexical databases, provides a partial sketch of the receiving culture. Agricultural vocabulary in Old Javanese inscriptions is overwhelmingly native: more than nine in ten economic terms have no Sanskrit source. Maritime vocabulary is more conserved in peripheral communities such as Bali and Tengger than in court centres. The word for "writing" itself, *surat*, is reconstructed to Proto-Austronesian, pre-dating any Indian contact by millennia.

The receiving culture was agricultural, maritime, and — if the word *surat* is any guide — already engaged with the concept of recording, even if the medium was organic rather than lithic. What Indianisation supplied was not the idea of inscription but a specific technology for it: Brahmi-derived script on imperishable stone.

*[Footnote: cite Zoetmulder 1982 on OJ vocabulary; Blust 1999 on PAN *surat; DHARMA project.]*

## 3 Why the Layer Is Invisible (~2,000 words)

### 3.1 The Taphonomic Filter

*[Narrative, NOT technical. Tell the candi stories.]*

Tell the story of Candi Sambisari: a farmer ploughing his field in 1966, the plough striking stone. Six and a half metres below the surface, a complete Hindu temple complex. Built around 835 CE. Nobody knew it was there.

*[If Sambisari was used as the opening hook (Option A), use a different entry point here — perhaps start with Candi Kedulan or the Dwarapala.]*

Tell the story of Candi Kedulan: discovered in 1993 during road construction. Seven metres down. Fourteen distinct layers of fluvio-volcanic sediment. A temple that had been invisible for a thousand years.

Tell the story of the Dwarapala of Singosari: half-buried when rediscovered in 1803. By 1903, the landscape had risen further around them. Measured rates: roughly 3.5 millimetres per year.

These are not exceptional events. They are the normal condition of volcanic Java. At measured rates of 2 to 6 millimetres per year, any archaeological deposit from before the fourth century of the common era now lies at depths of 6 to 10 metres — below the reach of surface survey, standard excavation, and most geophysical methods.

*[Footnote: cite Newhall et al. 2000 on Sambisari/Kedulan stratigraphy; Verbeek 1891 on Dwarapala.]*

### 3.2 The Inscription Bias

*[The epistemological problem: history = writing = Indian influence]*

The periodisation of Indonesian history follows a telling script. "Prehistoric" means before inscriptions. The "Hindu-Buddhist period" begins with the appearance of Sanskrit inscriptions in the fourth century. The "Islamic period" begins with the appearance of Islamic inscriptions. The "colonial period" begins with European records.

Each period begins when a new writing system arrives. The implication, seldom stated but structurally embedded, is that history begins with writing — and since writing in Java arrived with Indian contact, pre-Indian Java is by definition "pre-historical."

But this is a category error. The absence of writing is not the absence of history. It is the absence of a particular technology for recording history. Oral traditions, material culture, spatial practices, and ritual sequences are also historical records. They are, however, records that the colonial scholarly apparatus was not designed to read, and that the volcanic landscape is not designed to preserve.

### 3.3 The Colonial Gaze

*[Engage with Bloembergen and Eickhoff 2020, Sears 1996]*

Bloembergen and Eickhoff (2020) document how Dutch colonial archaeology in Indonesia was organised around the monumental: Hindu temples, Buddhist stupas, Islamic mosques. The Oudheidkundige Dienst (Archaeological Service, established 1913) inventoried, restored, and photographed stone structures. What was not stone was not archaeology.

This was not negligence — it was an epistemological framework. Stone endures; organic material does not. In volcanic Java, this bias is compounded: stone temples survive volcanic burial (they are found by farmers hitting stone with ploughs), while organic settlements are destroyed by the same process. The archaeological record that the colonial service constructed was the record of what stone survived volcanic burial — a doubly filtered dataset.

*[Footnote: Bloembergen's own article in BKI (2011) on how Prambanan was "conserved" as a national monument illustrates this dynamic perfectly.]*

Sears (1996) shows how Dutch scholars constructed "Javanese tradition" as a knowable, stable object by anchoring it to Indian textual sources: the Mahabharata, the Ramayana. What was not anchored to Indian texts was classified as "folk tradition" or "superstition" — analytically marginal. The slametan, Pranata Mangsa, the cult of *hyang* — these were noted by ethnographers but not theorised by historians, because they had no Indian textual anchor.

## 4 Traces in Language (~1,500 words)

*[Frame: Wolters asked what the local was. Language answers.]*

### 4.1 What Sanskrit Did Not Replace

When Sanskrit entered Javanese, it did not replace uniformly. Analysis of Old Javanese vocabulary reveals a domain-specific pattern. Agriculture: the overwhelming majority of terms are native Austronesian — wet rice cultivation, tools, seasons, crops. The economic base was entirely indigenous. Sanskrit contributed religious vocabulary for agricultural rituals but not for agriculture itself. Religion: the cosmological vocabulary was almost entirely replaced by Sanskrit. Daily life, material culture, body, kinship: predominantly native.

This is not "Indianisation" in any totalising sense. It is elite register overlay. The court adopted Sanskrit for prestige, ritual, and politics. The village continued speaking Austronesian for everything that mattered economically.

*[Footnote: Zoetmulder (1982); compare with similar domain-specific borrowing patterns in other contact situations — Norman French in English, Arabic in Malay/Indonesian.]*

### 4.2 What Peripheries Preserved

*[Engage with Fox 1993/2006 on Austronesian models]*

Comparative analysis of Austronesian vocabulary across more than 1,300 languages reveals that peripheral communities — Bali, Tengger, Madura — preserve more Proto-Malayo-Polynesian cognates than court centres such as Yogyakarta and Solo. The maritime vocabulary is especially telling: Balinese retains the Proto-Malayo-Polynesian word for "sea" (*laut*); standard Javanese replaced it.

The pattern is consistent with Wolters' localisation model: court centres localised Indian models intensively, peripheral communities less so. But it also reveals what was being localised into — an Austronesian cultural matrix with its own agricultural technology, maritime expertise, kinship systems, and cosmological vocabulary.

*[Footnote: Fox (2006) on "origin structures" in Austronesian societies — the receiving culture had its own models of social organisation, not a vacuum.]*

### 4.3 The Word *Surat*

The Proto-Austronesian root *\*surat* (writing, inscription, tattoo) is reconstructed to 3,000-5,000 years before the present — millennia before any Indian contact with the archipelago. This does not mean Austronesian societies had alphabetic writing. It means the concept of recording — marking, inscribing, encoding meaning on a surface — was indigenous. The Indian contribution was a specific technology (Brahmi-derived script on stone), not the idea itself.

*[Footnote: Blust and Trussel, Austronesian Comparative Dictionary, s.v. *surat. The word survives across the Austronesian world: Malay surat, Tagalog sulat, Malagasy soratra.]*

## 5 Traces in Ritual (~1,500 words)

*[Frame: Hertz (1907) + Javanese tradition's own words]*

### 5.1 The Slametan Persistence

*[Tell the story — not the statistics]*

The Javanese death ritual follows a sequence: three days, seven days, forty days, one hundred days, one thousand days. Four religious waves — Hindu-Buddhist, Islamic, Christian, nationalist — modified the content of prayers at each stage. None modified the timing. Muslim families recite *yasin* at *nyewu*; Catholic families recite rosaries; Kejawen families invoke *hyang*. The interval is the same.

This persistence is not stubbornness — it is calibration. The tradition itself is explicit: at forty days, the body's soft tissues are understood to have dissolved. At one thousand days, nothing remains. These are not metaphors. They are observations — generations of empirical knowledge about what happens to bodies buried in volcanic soil.

*[Footnote: Geertz (1960:71-2) records the slametan sequence. Beatty (1999:156-8) documents its multivocal persistence across religious boundaries. Hendrajaya et al. (2020) provides the most recent ethnographic data.]*

### 5.2 Hertz's Insight

Robert Hertz (1907) proposed that in societies practising secondary burial, the liminal period between death and final ceremony corresponds to the body's physical transformation. The soul cannot join the ancestors until the body has completed its dissolution. The social process mirrors the physical process.

The Javanese slametan is a primary-burial variant of Hertz's model: the soul's departure is tracked against the body's disappearance. What Hertz theorised abstractly, Javanese tradition states concretely — in its own vocabulary of flesh, bone, and dust.

### 5.3 The Pan-Austronesian Pattern

*[Engage with Bloch and Parry 1982, Metcalf and Huntington 1991]*

The multi-stage mortuary ritual with explicit body-soul parallelism is not uniquely Javanese. It appears across the Austronesian world: Toraja, Balinese *ngaben*, Malagasy *famadihana*, Philippine secondary burial. The pattern is pre-Hindu, pre-Islamic, pre-Christian. It belongs to the Austronesian cultural matrix — Lombard's missing layer.

## 6 Traces in Stone (~1,000 words)

### 6.1 Temple Siting as Environmental Knowledge

Hindu-Buddhist temples in Java cluster on the western slopes of volcanoes — the side sheltered from prevailing eruption patterns. This is not accidental. Temple builders chose sites that minimised volcanic risk while remaining close to volcanic fertility. The canonical orientation (east-facing, towards the equinox sunrise) was maintained regardless of volcanic position — the religious rule was followed, but the siting was pragmatic.

This pragmatic siting reveals indigenous environmental knowledge: communities knew which slopes were safe and which were dangerous. This knowledge pre-dates Indianisation — it would have been accumulated over generations of volcanic experience. The temples are Hindu; the knowledge of where to build them is not.

### 6.2 Pranata Mangsa

The Javanese agricultural calendar (Pranata Mangsa) divides the year into seasonal periods calibrated to local ecological conditions — not to the movements of the stars, but to the blooming of specific plants, the behaviour of certain animals, and the patterns of rainfall. Its temporal structure does not align with any Hindu, Islamic, or Western calendar system. It is indigenous — and it implicitly encodes volcanic hazard information through its attention to seasonal patterns of eruption and lahars.

*[Footnote: Daldjoeni (1984) on Pranata Mangsa; compare with Fox (2006) on Austronesian "calendrical knowledge" as a category of environmental expertise.]*

## 7 The Politics of the Invisible (~2,000 words) [HUMAN VOICE]

*[This is the section that makes the paper BKI-worthy. Must engage with social relevance. Write narratively, not as policy analysis.]*

### 7.1 Heritage Policy

If the foundational layer of Javanese civilisation lies underground — buried by volcanic sedimentation at depths of 6 to 10 metres — then Indonesian heritage policy is, by design, looking in the wrong place. The Oudheidkundige Dienst model (inventory, restore, display stone monuments) cannot access what is not on the surface. A heritage policy adequate to Java's actual history would require subsurface investigation at a scale Indonesia's archaeological infrastructure has never attempted.

*[Engage with Bloembergen and Eickhoff 2020 on how heritage policy reproduces colonial categories. Also cite Tanudirjo 1995 on Indonesian archaeology's colonial inheritance.]*

### 7.2 National Identity

Indonesian school textbooks teach that Indonesian "history" begins with the Hindu-Buddhist kingdoms — Kutai, Tarumanagara, Sriwijaya. The implication is that "civilisation" arrived from outside — from India. The pre-Hindu period is *prasejarah* — literally, before history.

But this periodisation is an artefact of what survived the taphonomic filter. Kutai's Yupa pillars were carved in stone in volcanic-free Kalimantan — they survived because there was nothing to bury them. If equivalent inscriptions existed in volcanic Java, they would now lie under 7 to 10 metres of sediment. The "oldest kingdom" is the most visible one, not necessarily the oldest one.

What does it mean for national identity if the "beginning of civilisation" narrative is a visibility artefact?

### 7.3 Decolonial Practice

*[Engage with Chakrabarty 2000, Tanudirjo 1995]*

The colonial periodisation — "prehistoric," "Hindu-Buddhist," "Islamic," "colonial," "national" — persists in Indonesian academia because the alternative is literally buried. Decolonising Indonesian historiography cannot succeed through textual revision alone. It requires digging — physically and metaphorically.

The irony: volcanic burial that hides the past may also preserve it. Sealed, anaerobic contexts beneath volcanic sediment may contain organic materials that surface contexts destroy. The invisible layer may be the best-preserved layer — if we can reach it.

### 7.4 The Ethics of Absence

There is a moral dimension to the historiographic invisibility of pre-Hindu Java. To accept the standard periodisation uncritically is to accept, implicitly, that Javanese civilisation began when India arrived — that the local contribution was passive, receptive, pre-historical. Wolters' localisation corrected this at the theoretical level. The taphonomic argument corrects it at the empirical level: there was a receiving culture, it was complex, and its invisibility is a geophysical accident, not a cultural fact.

*[Footnote: This argument has parallels in Australian Indigenous archaeology (where colonial "terra nullius" ignored 65,000 years of occupation) and in African archaeology (where the equation of "civilisation" with literacy marginalised pre-literate societies). The mechanisms differ — no volcanoes in Australia — but the epistemological structure is similar: absence of a SPECIFIC TYPE of evidence was mistaken for absence of history itself.]*

## 8 Conclusion: The Carrefour Has a Basement (~500 words) [HUMAN VOICE]

Lombard's metaphor of the crossroads is apt — but every crossroads has a surface. What lies beneath?

This paper has argued that the deepest visible layer of Lombard's *carrefour* — the Indian heritage — is not the deepest layer. Beneath it lies an Austronesian stratum whose material remains have been buried by volcanic sedimentation and whose historiographic traces have been marginalised by the equation of "history" with "writing" and "civilisation" with "India."

We have not found this layer. We have argued that it exists, that its invisibility has structural causes, and that traces of it remain legible in language (agricultural vocabulary that Sanskrit never replaced, the concept of *surat*), in ritual (the slametan sequence calibrated to decomposition in volcanic soil), and in spatial practice (temple siting that encodes volcanic awareness).

We offer no discovery claim. We offer a visibility claim — an argument about why the evidence is absent, what it would look like if found, and where to look for it. Whether what we predict is there requires not only new excavation methods but a historiographic framework willing to recognise history in forms other than writing, and civilisation in forms other than monuments.

The *carrefour* has a basement. The door is buried, but we know where to dig.
