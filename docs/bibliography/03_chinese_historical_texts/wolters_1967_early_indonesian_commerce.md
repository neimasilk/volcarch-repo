# Notes

## Core claim (1 sentence)
Chinese dynastic histories (Hou Han Shu, Liang Shu, New Tang Shu, etc.) attest to a sequence of organised Indonesian polities trading with China from the 2nd century CE onwards — Ye-tiao (Java, 132 CE), Ko-ying (Sumatra, 3rd century), Kan-t'o-li (Sumatra, 5th-6th century), then Srivijaya — establishing that Nusantara political complexity was recognised by external observers long before the Hindu-Buddhist monumental record emerges in the archaeology.

## Key findings relevant to VOLCARCH

### The Ye-tiao / Yavadvipa attestation (132 CE)
- **Hou Han Shu** records an embassy in 132 CE from "Tiao Pien" (possibly Deva Varman), king of **Ye-tiao** (葉調).
- Wolters' Appendix "Yeh-t'iao and P'i-ch'ien" argues Ye-tiao = Yavadvipa = Java, following Pelliot (1916).
- Pelliot's identification is based on Late Middle Chinese phonology: 葉調 ≈ [jiap dɛwh] matches Sanskrit Yava-(d)vipa.
- Implication: a Javanese polity sufficiently organised to dispatch an international embassy to Han China existed by 132 CE — approximately 250 years before Kutai's Yupa inscriptions (the conventional "oldest Indonesian kingdom").

### Ko-ying (3rd century Sumatra)
- Attested as a trading kingdom on the Sumatra coast in the 3rd century CE.
- Not on the direct India-China sea route but a node in western Indonesian commerce.
- Documented in Chinese sources (Sanguozhi period records).

### Kan-t'o-li (5th-6th century Sumatra)
- Leading commercial kingdom on Sumatra coast 5th-6th c. CE.
- First tributary missions from Indonesian kingdoms to China documented in this period.
- Precedes and overlaps with the rise of Srivijaya.

### Sequential attestation
Ye-tiao (Java, 132 CE) → Ko-ying (Sumatra, 3rd c.) → Kan-t'o-li (Sumatra, 5th-6th c.) → Malayu (Jambi) → Srivijaya (7th c.).

This is an almost continuous 500-year textual attestation of organised Nusantara polities in Chinese records, predating and extending through the period VOLCARCH claims as "invisible civilisation."

## How it relates to VOLCARCH
**Massive supporting evidence.** The invisible-civilisation thesis has been confronted with the question: "If there was organised civilisation pre-400 CE in Nusantara, why don't we see it?" The Chinese textual attestation answers: external observers DID see it, recorded it, and received ambassadors from it. The archaeological invisibility is a function of taphonomy in Java/Sumatra, not a function of non-existence.

Specifically:
- Channel 3 in P0: Chinese historical texts = independent observer community attesting to complex polity in Java (Ye-tiao 132 CE) long before Hindu-Buddhist monumental archaeology
- §2.2 demographic puzzle: organised states require population bases in the ranges VOLCARCH estimates
- Addresses the "if it existed why no one saw it" counter — CHINA SAW IT, wrote about it, received embassies.

## Methodological strengths
- Canonical monograph; cited in virtually every subsequent synthesis on early Indonesian history
- Primary source use (Chinese dynastic histories translated and analyzed)
- Cross-references with Indonesian archaeology where possible
- Pelliot's phonological identification (1916) is independent philological work

## Methodological weaknesses / critiques
- Attribution of specific Chinese toponyms to specific Indonesian locations is contested by minority scholars
- 1967 monograph; some specifics updated by later work (Hall, Manguin)
- Wolters elsewhere warns against over-reading Chinese records as ethnographically precise

## Chronological implications
- **132 CE Ye-tiao embassy = pre-Kutai Javanese polity attestation**
- Extends the documented civilisation horizon back ~270 years from Kutai (400 CE)
- Not a marginal hypothesis — Wolters + Pelliot are foundational for the field
