## Review
**Referee Report for "Multi-Site Calibration of Volcanic Sedimentation Rates and Implications for Archaeological Visibility in Java, Indonesia"**

As a senior Southeast Asian archaeologist with 25 years of field experience on Java, including excavations at Trowulan, Penanggungan, and Kedulan (one of the authors' calibration sites), I approach this manuscript with a deep understanding of the Javanese archaeological landscape and a healthy skepticism towards computational claims that do not directly engage with the material record. My assessment is that the archaeological record must constrain theory, not the other way around.

**1. SUMMARY**
This manuscript presents a computational analysis aiming to quantify cumulative volcanic sedimentation rates across Java. Using four monumental stone sites (Dwarapala, Sambisari, Kedulan, Kimpulan) and an unspecified "51-pair eruption--site dataset," the authors derive a mean aggradation rate of 4.4 Â± 1.2 mm/yr. They project that this rate implies significant burial depths (meters) for pre-10th century archaeological deposits, exceeding conventional surface survey and ground-penetrating radar limits. The paper suggests that the observed sparsity of older sites in Java is consistent with taphonomic erasure, framing this as a methodological foundation for targeted subsurface investigation.

**2. OVERALL ASSESSMENT**
Reject.

**3. MAJOR CONCERNS**

1.  **Circular Reasoning and Unsubstantiated Theoretical Framing:** The paper's stated scope is "narrowly geomorphological" (Abstract), yet it is explicitly framed by the "invisible civilization" hypothesis, which is relegated to a "companion synthesis (Amien, in preparation)" (Sect. 1, 2.2, 2.4, 5.3). This creates a fundamental problem of circular reasoning: the current paper purports to quantify a process that then "explains" an archaeological absence, which is itself the premise of the companion paper. This is not how archaeological science should proceed. A geomorphological study should stand on its own merits, with archaeological implications presented as testable hypotheses, not as explanations for a pre-determined theoretical outcome.
    *   *Why it matters:* This approach biases the interpretation of limited data and presents a model as confirmation of a pre-existing, unsubstantiated theory rather than an objective analysis. It undermines the scientific rigor of the entire enterprise.
    *   *How to address:* The paper must be entirely decoupled from the "invisible civilization" narrative. All references to the companion synthesis and any claims about explaining the "pre-10th century absence" must be removed. If the authors wish to publish a geomorphological calibration, it must be presented as such, with archaeological implications framed strictly as hypotheses for future, *empirical* testing.

2.  **Inadequate and Imprecise Calibration Data for Archaeological Contexts:**
    *   **Dwarapala Anchor (Sect. 3.2, 5.4):** The primary empirical anchor for the Kelud system, the Dwarapala statues, relies on a colonial-era observation by Nicolaus Engelhard in 1803 that "approximately half" of each statue was buried. This is a highly imprecise and anecdotal measurement for a foundational calibration point. The authors themselves admit that "The precise archival source requires confirmation against the original report" (Sect. 5.4). This admission is fatal to the reliability of this calibration point. My experience with colonial-era reports suggests such observations are often qualitative and lack the precision required for quantitative geoarchaeological modeling.
    *   **Monumental Bias (Sect. 3.3, 5.3, 5.4):** All four primary calibration points (Dwarapala, Sambisari, Kedulan, Kimpulan) are monumental stone temples. The paper acknowledges this limitation (Sect. 5.4) but then makes a significant leap by assuming transferability to "open habitation sites" (Sect. 5.3), stating that the "51-pair eruption--site dataset, which includes non-monumental contexts... suggests that the monument-to-settlement transfer is reasonable." This is a critical, unsubstantiated assumption. Monumental structures, often built on platforms or with deep foundations, interact with depositional and erosional processes very differently from ephemeral, organic-material settlements. Their sheer mass and verticality can act as sediment traps or deflectors, and their original ground surface is often complex to determine stratigraphically. Having supervised excavations at Kedulan, I can attest to the intricate stratigraphic challenges in determining the precise original ground level relative to the temple's construction.
    *   *Why it matters:* The accuracy and representativeness of the calibration rates are paramount. If the input data are fundamentally imprecise or unrepresentative of the archaeological contexts the paper purports to explain (i.e., settlements), then all subsequent projections and conclusions are unreliable.
    *   *How to address:* The Dwarapala calibration point must be removed unless rigorous, primary archival research can provide a precise, quantifiable measurement. The assumption of transferability from monuments to settlements must be removed or supported by extensive, direct geoarchaeological evidence from *known, non-monumental settlement sites* with clear stratigraphic sequences and datable material. Without such data, the paper cannot make claims about the burial of "pre-monumental Javanese settlement."

3.  **Lack of Transparency and Detail for "Independent Validation" Dataset (Sect. 3.4, 4.2):** The "51-pair eruption--site correlation dataset" is presented as an "independent check" and "independent confirmation." However, the description is extremely vague. No details are provided on *which* sites, *which* eruptions, *how* the "physically measured burial depths" were obtained, or *how* the "760-year horizon" for this dataset was determined. The claim of "agreement within 3%" between the model and this dataset is meaningless without full transparency.
    *   *Why it matters:* An independent validation must be fully transparent and reproducible. Without the underlying data and methodology, this section functions as an assertion rather than verifiable evidence, undermining the credibility of the entire study.
    *   *How to address:* The full 51-pair dataset, including site names, eruption events, dates, measured depths, and primary sources, must be provided as a supplementary table. The methodology for calculating the "760-year horizon" for this dataset must be explicitly detailed.

4.  **Inadequate Treatment of Compaction and Episodic Deposition (Sect. 3.5, 5.4):** The paper acknowledges that the linear model is a "first-order approximation" and that "unconsolidated volcanic sediment compacts over time by 10--30% at depths exceeding roughly 5m" (Sect. 5.4). However, the burial depth projections in Table 2 are presented as direct linear extrapolations without incorporating compaction. The statement that "realised depths likely somewhat shallower for the oldest eras" is a significant caveat that is not quantitatively integrated into the model. Similarly, the episodic nature of volcanic deposition is noted but not modeled, with the "wide rate range" (2.4-6.2 mm/yr) offered as a partial capture of variability.
    *   *Why it matters:* Compaction is a known and significant geological process that affects sediment thickness over centuries. Ignoring it in the primary projections, especially for the critical pre-Hindu era (3.9-10.1m), renders the depth estimates less accurate and potentially misleading, particularly when discussing detection limits for GPR. The linear model is a gross oversimplification for a complex, episodic process.
    *   *How to address:* The model should incorporate a compaction factor, or the projections should be presented as a range that explicitly accounts for compaction, rather than just a linear extrapolation with a qualitative caveat. A more sophisticated, non-linear model that attempts to account for episodic deposition or at least its statistical properties would be more appropriate for multi-century projections.

5.  **Misinterpretation and Methodological Flaws in Spatial Analysis (Sect. 3.6, 4.4, 5.3, 5.4):** The spatial analysis of known sites, while acknowledged to be biased by survey intensity and monument durability, is still used to infer that the "volcanic deficit is not reducible to differential survey effort alone" (Sect. 5.3). This is a contradictory and problematic inference. If the dataset is fundamentally biased towards monumental stone sites discovered in specific survey areas, then any residual correlation, even after controlling for terrain suitability, cannot be reliably interpreted as evidence for taphonomic effects on *settlement* distribution. The strong spatial autocorrelation (Moran's I = 0.937) is acknowledged as a concern (Sect. 5.4) but not addressed in the analysis, rendering the reported p-values for correlations (e.g., $\rho = -0.955, p = 0.0008$) unreliable and inflated.
    *   *Why it matters:* The spatial analysis, despite caveats, subtly reinforces the taphonomic argument using a dataset that is fundamentally unsuitable for this purpose. It risks misinterpreting the distribution of *discovered monuments* as evidence for *buried settlements*. The unaddressed spatial autocorrelation invalidates the statistical significance claims.
    *   *How to address:* The spatial analysis should be presented purely as a descriptive overview of the *known, biased* archaeological record. Any inferential claims about a "volcanic deficit" or the irreducibility to survey effort must be removed. If such claims are to be made, a formal spatial regression model that accounts for spatial autocorrelation is required, and the dataset itself would need to be demonstrably representative of past settlement, which it is not.

**4. METHODOLOGICAL CONCERNS**

*   **Are statistical methods appropriate?** The use of Spearman's rank correlation for the spatial analysis is appropriate for the type of data, but the small number of distance bands (n=7) makes the p-value highly sensitive. The lack of detail on the "residual correlation" and "quasi-Poisson likelihood ratio test" (Sect. 5.3) in the methods section (Sect. 3.6) is a transparency issue. Most critically, the acknowledged strong spatial autocorrelation (Moran's I = 0.937) is not addressed in the statistical analysis, rendering the reported p-values for correlations invalid.
*   **Is the evidence multi-channel or is there hidden dependence?** The four calibration sites are presented as distinct systems, but the method of calibration (depth/time from monuments) is consistent. The "independent validation" dataset's independence is questionable due to lack of transparency; if it relies on similar colonial-era observations or BPCB reports, it may share similar biases. The spatial analysis uses the same biased archaeological dataset that the taphonomic argument seeks to explain, thus lacking independence.
*   **Are the comparisons controlled for confounders?** The spatial analysis attempts to control for terrain suitability, but as noted, the inherent biases of the archaeological dataset and unaddressed spatial autocorrelation limit its effectiveness. The brief mention of Kutai (Sect. 2.4) is anecdotal and not integrated into a controlled comparison.
*   **Is the falsification criterion actually falsifying?** The paper's model is primarily explanatory, seeking to account for an observed absence. While finding sites at predicted depths would support the model, the failure to find them could be attributed to actual absence, deeper burial, or GPR limitations, making it difficult to truly falsify the core premise of deep burial.
*   **Are the computational methods transparent and reproducible?** The use of OpenStreetMap, Wikidata, and a GitHub repository for data and scripts is commendable and promotes reproducibility. However, the lack of detail for the "51-pair eruption--site dataset" and the specific statistical models used for residual analysis (e.g., quasi-Poisson) reduces overall transparency.

**5. SPECIFIC CLAIMS TO CHALLENGE**

*   **"Multi-Site Calibration of Volcanic Sedimentation Rates... establishes a mean cumulative aggradation rate of 4.4 Â± 1.2 mm/yr" (Abstract):** Plausible but under-supported. The sample size (N=4) is too small for such a broad claim across "two volcanic systems and 350km of Java." The precision of the Dwarapala anchor is highly questionable, and the transferability from monuments to settlements is an unproven assumption.
*   **"Applied as a first-order linear projection, this rate places Kanjuruhan-era... deposits at 3.0--7.9m and pre-400 CE deposits at 3.9--10.1m depth..." (Abstract):** Overreach. The linear projection ignores the significant effect of compaction, which the authors acknowledge. The wide range, while reflecting uncertainty, also highlights the imprecision of the underlying data and model.
*   **"A 51-pair eruption--site dataset... yields a mean measured depth of 3.41m, consistent with the rate model over a 760-year horizon." (Abstract):** Under-supported. The lack of transparency regarding this dataset prevents any meaningful evaluation of its independence or the validity of the comparison.
*   **"Spatial analysis of 666 East Java sites shows near-volcano clustering that reflects survey intensity and monument durability rather than settlement distribution." (Abstract):** Plausible but under-supported. While the interpretation of bias is likely correct, the analysis itself is compromised by unaddressed spatial autocorrelation and the inherent bias of the dataset, making it difficult to draw firm conclusions about what it *doesn't* reflect.
*   **"The cross-system convergence is the central finding." (Abstract):** Overreach. With N=4 and the issues with the Dwarapala data, claiming "convergence" as a central finding is premature. The Merapi sites are consistently higher than Kelud, which is noted, but the "convergence" is then used to justify a single mean rate.
*   **"The archaeological invisibility of pre-monumental Javanese settlement is consistent with a geomorphologically plausible rate of cumulative burial..." (Conclusions):** Overreach. This is the core "invisible civilization" claim. While "consistent" is a weak word, the paper frames this as an explanation rather than a hypothesis. The plausibility of the rate is undermined by the data quality and methodological issues.

**6. WHAT THE PAPER DOES WELL**

1.  **Highlights a critical geoarchaeological problem:** The paper correctly identifies the significant challenge of archaeological visibility in volcanic landscapes, particularly in Java, and the potential for taphonomic bias to obscure older, non-monumental sites. This is a genuinely important area for future geoarchaeological research in the region.
2.  **Attempts multi-site calibration:** The effort to move beyond a single site and establish a regional rate, even with a small and problematic sample, represents a conceptual step towards understanding broader landscape processes.
3.  **Transparency of computational resources:** The commitment to making data and scripts available on GitHub is commendable and aligns with best practices for reproducible computational research.

**7. WHAT THE PAPER SHOULD BE, IF NOT THIS**

A more modest, rigorous, and acceptable version of this paper would be a focused geoarchaeological study that:
*   **Removes all references to "invisible civilization" and companion papers.** It must be a purely methodological paper, devoid of speculative archaeological claims.
*   **Focuses on a single, well-documented volcanic system (e.g., Merapi) with robust, primary geoarchaeological data.** The Dwarapala data is too weak for a primary anchor.
*   **Conducts new, targeted geoarchaeological fieldwork (e.g., coring, deep GPR, or excavation) at *known, non-monumental settlement sites* with clear stratigraphic sequences and datable material.** This would provide direct, empirical evidence of burial rates for habitation sites, rather than relying on monumental architecture.
*   **Develops a more sophisticated sedimentation model that explicitly accounts for compaction, episodic deposition, and varying landscape positions (basins vs. slopes).**
*   **Presents the spatial analysis purely as a descriptive overview of the *known, biased* archaeological record, with a strong emphasis on the limitations imposed by survey bias and monumentality, rather than using it to infer taphonomic effects on *settlement* distribution.**
*   **Concludes with a call for specific, targeted geoarchaeological investigations to *test* the hypothesis of deep burial, rather than asserting it as an explanation for the current archaeological record.**

**8. ONE QUESTION FOR THE AUTHORS**

Given the acknowledged limitations of using monumental stone architecture for calibrating general settlement burial rates, and the imprecision of the Dwarapala anchor, what specific, new geoarchaeological fieldwork (e.g., targeted coring, deep GPR, or excavation at known, non-monumental sites with clear stratigraphic sequences and datable material) would you propose to rigorously test your projected burial depths for pre-10th century habitation sites in Java?
