## Context

P11 v0.2 argues that candi siting patterns encode ancient Javanese knowledge of volcanic hazards. The ADV-2 result (E081) reinforces the need to ground L1 claims in burial depth evidence rather than site-type ratios. P11 benefits from three recent experiments that provide spatial, temporal, and physical evidence layers.

## New Evidence from E082: Inscription Spatial Data

### What E082 provides

182 georeferenced DHARMA inscriptions (out of 268 total), 175 in Java/Bali. This is the first spatially resolved inscription dataset for Indonesia.

### Key finding: Inscription-Candi Spatial Divergence

| Dataset | Mean dist. to volcano | Median | N |
|---------|----------------------|--------|---|
| Candi (E065) | 16.5 km | 14.7 km | 142 |
| Inscriptions (E082) | 25.5 km | 27.6 km | 175 |

**Inscriptions are 9.0 km farther from volcanoes than candi.** This divergence is significant because:

1. **Temples were deliberately placed near volcanoes** — consistent with P11's claim that temple siting reflects volcanic landscape awareness. Candi cluster at 10-20 km, the agriculturally productive volcanic-flank zone.

2. **Inscriptions reflect administrative geography, not ritual geography.** Charter grants describe villages, rice fields, and market transactions in the broader hinterland. Their spatial distribution represents the inhabited landscape, not the sacred landscape.

3. **Different taphonomic processes.** Candi are monumental stone structures partially resistant to burial (they protrude or are rediscovered through erosion). Inscriptions on copper plates or stone slabs are smaller and more easily lost. The spatial divergence may partly reflect differential survival — candi near volcanoes were impressive enough to be rediscovered, while smaller inscriptions in the volcanic proximal zone may be buried.

A figure showing the dual distribution: candi distance (peaking at ~15 km) vs inscription distance (peaking at ~28 km). This visually demonstrates that temple siting is a DISTINCT spatial behavior from general settlement, supporting the "informedness" interpretation.

## New Evidence from E083: Tephra-Archaeological Correlation

### What E083 provides

51 eruption-site pairs documenting specific volcanic events affecting specific archaeological sites. 24 sites with physically measured burial depths.

### Relevance to P11

P11 argues that ancient Javanese people were "informed" about volcanic hazards. E083 shows they had reason to be:

- **Kelud 1919:** 7 archaeological sites affected in a single eruption. Colonial reports document lahar burying temples to 1.5-2.0 m.
- **Merapi ongoing:** 15 sites documented with burial effects. Sambisari (5.0 m), Kedulan (2.7 m), Liangan (4.0 m).
- **Prambanan zone:** Maximum documented burial of 9.14 m.

If volcanic burial was this severe in the historical and colonial periods, classical-period communities certainly experienced similar events and would have had strong incentives to encode volcanic knowledge in temple siting.

## E082 Century-Level Findings

### C9 inscriptions are closest to volcanoes (13.0 km mean)

This is the Mataram dynasty peak — the period of Borobudur, Prambanan, and the Kedu Plain concentration near Merapi/Merbabu. For P11, this means:

- The period of greatest volcanic proximity in the inscription record is ALSO the period of greatest temple construction
- Mataram court proximity to Merapi was deliberate, not accidental
- Volcanic knowledge would have been most acute during this period

### Post-929 CE eastward shift

C11-C12 inscriptions average 42-50 km from nearest volcano, consistent with the move to East Java (Kediri period). P11 can note that the spatial shift in political center also changed the relationship between inscriptions/temples and volcanic landscapes.

## Integration with ADV-2 Lesson

P11 should NOT argue that volcanic regions have different site-type distributions. Instead, the argument is:

1. **Depth evidence** (E070, E075, E083) proves volcanic burial is real and severe
2. **Candi spatial patterns** (E065) show temples were deliberately placed in volcanic landscapes
3. **Inscription spatial patterns** (E082) show administrative geography differs from ritual geography
4. **The divergence** between temple and inscription siting suggests intentional landscape choices by temple builders, consistent with volcanic informedness

The P11 argument is strengthened by the ADV-2 result because it clarifies that the interesting signal is not "what survives" (site-type ratios) but "where people chose to build" (spatial siting patterns). Temple siting is a behavioral signal, not a taphonomic artifact.

## Specific Sections of P11 v0.2 to Update

1. **Section on taphonomic context** — Add E083 burial depth table. Frame volcanic hazard as the known environmental context in which temple siting decisions were made.
2. **Section on spatial analysis** — Add inscription comparison from E082. Show that candi-volcano proximity is NOT shared by inscriptions, strengthening the argument that temple siting is specifically directed toward volcanic landscapes.

4. **Conclusion** — Note that the inscription spatial data enables future research on differential taphonomic processes (monuments vs small artifacts vs organic remains in volcanic vs non-volcanic zones).

## ADV-3 Relevance to P11

The ADV-3 result (volcanic proximity beta = -0.477, p = 0.0015 after survey intensity control) is directly relevant to P11:

- It confirms that the archaeological site deficit near volcanoes is real, not an artifact of survey effort
- P11 can cite this as evidence that volcanic landscapes genuinely have fewer DISCOVERED sites, making temple siting patterns even more informative — temples are among the few things we DO find in volcanic zones, precisely because they are monumental enough to resist complete burial
