## 1. What Makes P9 Different

P9 proposes the **Peripheral Conservatism Framework (PCF)** — a general methodology for recovering pre-Hindu substrates from living peripheral communities rather than from archaeological excavation at overwritten centres. It identifies three types of peripheral archive (geographic, political, temporal) and demonstrates the framework through four independent evidence streams: lexical cognacy, ritual structure, botanical practice, and epigraphic material culture. Madagascar serves as the key **temporal calibration point** (~1200 CE terminus ante quem).

P9 is unique because it is the only paper in the project that:
- Proposes a **general-purpose recovery framework** (PCF) applicable beyond any single evidence domain
- Uses **Madagascar as a dateable calibration instrument** with the Panji-absence test (E034)
- Establishes the **scale paradox** (Tengger drift vs Bali/Madagascar conservation) as a constraint on peripheral conservatism
- Integrates **botanical evidence** (Canarium GBIF distribution, Plumeria New World correction) as an independent data stream
- Addresses a **Southeast Asian studies generalist audience** with a multi-domain consilience argument

## 2. P9 vs P5: Different Questions, Different Evidence

### The Question
- **P5 asks:** Why does the Javanese slametan follow the specific sequence 3-7-40-100-1000 days? (Causal mechanism)
- **P9 asks:** Where in the Nusantaran landscape do pre-Hindu substrates survive, and how can we systematically recover them? (Methodological framework)

### The DHARMA Overlap
Both papers cite the DHARMA inscription corpus, but for entirely different purposes:
- **P5** screens 268 inscriptions for **ritual vocabulary** (hyang 43%, manghuri 10%, wuku 2%) to argue that indigenous cosmological terms survived Indianization, supporting the pre-Indic origin of slametan timing.
- **P9** uses the same corpus to calculate **material culture ratios** (63.4% organic vs 27.2% lithic in E040) to argue that pre-Hindu material culture was predominantly non-permanent and therefore archaeologically invisible.

The same dataset is interrogated with different keyword ontologies for different analytical purposes. No tables, figures, or statistical tests are shared between the two papers.

## 3. P9 vs P8: Different Scope, Different Methods

### The Question
- **P8 asks:** Can machine learning detect phonological non-conformity in Austronesian vocabulary, and does the detected non-mainstream layer cohere as a shared substrate? (Methods development)
- **P9 asks:** Where do pre-Hindu substrates survive in the landscape, and what multi-domain evidence demonstrates peripheral conservation? (Framework application)

### The Linguistic Overlap
Both papers discuss "substrate" in Austronesian languages and use ABVD data. However:
- **P8** develops a **novel ML pipeline** (XGBoost, 26 features, SHAP) to detect phonological non-conformity in **Sulawesi** languages. Its central finding is a negative result: non-mainstream forms do not cohere as shared word families (p = 0.569).
- **P9** uses **simple cognacy counting** (no machine learning) to compare PMP retention rates across **Java, Bali, Tengger, and Madagascar**. Its central finding is positive: large peripheries conserve while small isolates drift.

The two papers have **zero overlap in primary linguistic datasets**: P8 uses Muna, Bugis, Makassar, Wolio, Toraja-Sa'dan, and Tolaki (Sulawesi); P9 uses Javanese, Balinese, Tengger, Malagasy, Old Javanese, PMP, and PAn (Western Malayo-Polynesian).

## 4. Template Response to Reviewer

> **If a reviewer asks:** "This paper seems to overlap with companion papers on volcanic ritual timing [P5] and/or phonological substrate detection [P8]. Is this salami-slicing?"

**Suggested response:**

"Thank you for raising this important concern. This paper exists within a broader research programme on pre-Hindu Nusantaran substrates, but it is substantively independent of both companion papers.

**Regarding [P5]:** That paper asks why the Javanese slametan follows the specific numerical sequence 3-7-40-100-1000 days, and answers with forensic taphonomy (decomposition rates in volcanic Andosol). This paper [P9] asks where pre-Hindu substrates survive in the landscape and proposes the Peripheral Conservatism Framework — a general recovery methodology tested through four evidence domains. Although both papers reference the DHARMA inscription corpus, they extract different information: [P5] screens for ritual vocabulary (hyang frequency), while this paper calculates organic-vs-lithic material culture ratios. No statistical tests, tables, or figures are shared. The papers are submitted to different journals — [P5] to BKI (ethnolinguistic audience) and this paper to JSEAS (Southeast Asian studies audience) — reflecting their different disciplinary contributions.

**Regarding [P8]:** That paper develops and validates a machine learning pipeline for detecting phonological non-conformity in Sulawesi Austronesian languages. This paper's linguistic component uses simple cognacy-rate comparison (no machine learning) across Java, Bali, Tengger, and Madagascar — a completely different geographic scope with zero overlap in primary language datasets. [P8]'s central finding is negative (non-mainstream forms do not cohere as shared word families); this paper's central finding is positive (large peripheries conserve while small isolates drift). The papers are submitted to Oceanic Linguistics (computational linguistics audience) and JSEAS respectively.

The three papers form a complementary programme: [P8] builds the detection tool, [P5] identifies a specific ritual mechanism, and this paper provides the overarching framework. Each stands alone and makes an independent contribution to its target discipline. We are happy to provide all manuscripts for editorial review."
