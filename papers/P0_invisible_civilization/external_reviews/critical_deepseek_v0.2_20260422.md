**Manuscript Title:** The Invisible Civilisation: Six Independent Lines of Evidence for an Archaeologically Erased Pre-Hindu Nusantara
**Authors:** Mukhlis Amien & Go Frendi Gunawan
**Target Journal:** *Journal of Anthropological Archaeology*

---

### 1. SUMMARY
The manuscript argues that the apparent absence of pre-4th century CE archaeological sites in the volcanic interior of Java is not evidence of a sparse population or late state formation, but the result of a compound taphonomic process termed "selective survival." The authors propose that a substantial substrate civilization (estimated at 1-2 million people) existed but its organic settlement matrix has been rendered archaeologically invisible due to deep burial by volcanic sedimentation. This central claim is supported by the purported convergence of six independent evidence channels: 1) volcanic sedimentation rate calibration, 2) comparative archaeology with the Philippines, 3) linguistic substrate reconstruction, 4) reinterpretation of paleogenomic data, 5) colonial archive text mining, and 6) archaeometric evidence from exported Javanese material culture (glass beads, bronze drums). The paper's central contribution is a methodological framework for diagnosing "invisible civilizations" through multi-channel computational analysis, presented as a challenge to conventional developmental narratives in Southeast Asian archaeology.

### 2. OVERALL ASSESSMENT
**Reject.** The paper's core argument is an extraordinary claim built on a foundation of methodological overreach, evidentiary misrepresentation, and a fundamental disregard for the primary archaeological record. While it raises a provocative and potentially important taphonomic question, its execution is fatally flawed. The "convergence" of evidence is largely illusory, constructed through selective reading, inappropriate comparisons, and the conflation of absence-of-evidence with evidence-of-absence (or in this case, evidence-of-presence-buried-deeply). It substitutes computational complexity for archaeological rigor.

### 3. MAJOR CONCERNS
1.  **The Demographie Puzzle is Built on Circular Logic and Inappropriate Analogies (Section 2).** The entire argument hinges on the claim that Java *must* have had a population of 1-2 million by 400 CE. This estimate is derived from:
    *   **Carrying Capacity Models:** Applying generalized Austronesian population densities (5-30 persons/km²) to a derived "habitable area" is not an independent prediction but a theoretical maximum under optimal conditions. It assumes full utilization of the landscape for intensive agriculture, for which there is **no archaeological evidence** in pre-400 CE volcanic Java. Using figures from Bellwood (2017) and Kirch (2000), which often derive from later, more complex societies, to model a hypothetical earlier population is anachronistic.
    *   **Back-Projection from 1600 CE:** Using Reid's (1988) early modern figures and applying a uniform growth rate over 1200 years is historically naive. It ignores the documented transformative impacts of the "Indianization" process, including technological introductions (iron, plow agriculture, water management) and political centralization that would have dramatically increased carrying capacity *after* the 4th century CE. To use the post-transformation population to argue for a pre-transformation population of similar scale is circular.
    *   **The Ye-tiao/Yavadvipa Embassy (132 CE):** Citing this as evidence for a "polity" of substantial scale is a classic overinterpretation of a single, phonologically debated textual reference. An embassy could be organized by a coastal trading chiefdom with a relatively small demographic base, as known from other parts of Island Southeast Asia. It does not "constrain the lower bound" of population to anywhere near the paper's estimates.
    *   **The "Gap" Calculation is Meaningless:** Computing a "gap" of 1000- to 6500-fold between "expected" and "observed" settlements is pseudo-quantification. The "expected" number is a product of the flawed demographic model. If the premise is wrong, the gap is an artifact, not a puzzle.

2.  **The "Six Independent Channels" are Neither Independent nor Convincing.** The paper's central rhetorical device is fatally compromised.
    *   **Channel 1 (Sedimentation):** The calibration (P1-core) may be geomorphologically sound, but it establishes only a *potential* for deep burial. It is not evidence *for* buried settlements. The authors admit this but then treat it as the first pillar of convergence. A detection horizon is not evidence of what lies beneath it.
    *   **Channel 2 (Philippines Comparison):** This is an uncontrolled, misleading analogy. The authors claim to control for "climate, Austronesian heritage, tropical ecology, [and] organic architecture tradition," but this is superficial. The critical differences are not just volcanism and karst. The Philippines had a profoundly different cultural trajectory, with early and sustained Neolithic settlement evidence (e.g., the Batanes, Cagayan Valley, Batangas jade) that is **attested in the archaeological record**. Java's volcanic interior lacks this *entire sequence*. To compare a region with a rich, documented pre-400 CE record to one without, and conclude the difference is solely due to taphonomy, ignores the null hypothesis: **different historical trajectories**. The comparison to non-volcanic West Java (Buni, Batujaya) is more telling, but it actually undermines the thesis: these areas *do* show pre-400 CE archaeology. Why would a hypothesized vast interior civilization leave no trace in these adjacent, non-volcanic zones through trade, diffusion, or expansion? The "zero vs. hundreds" contrast is not a controlled experiment; it is a comparison of two distinct archaeological realities.
    *   **Channel 3 (Linguistics):** The linguistic evidence is interesting but irrelevant to the core archaeological claim. The existence of Proto-Austronesian vocabulary for writing (*surat*) does not prove writing was practiced in pre-4th century Java. A domain gradient of Sanskrit loanwords in Old Javanese texts is a well-known feature of language contact and says nothing about the size or complexity of the pre-contact society. A substrate lexicon in Sulawesi languages is evidence for pre-Austronesian populations in Sulawesi, not Java. This channel demonstrates language depth and contact phenomena, which are uncontroversial, but it is presented as if it supports a specific, large-scale, *material* civilization in Java. It does not.
    *   **Channel 4 (Paleogenomics):** This is a profound misrepresentation of the genetic data and its taphonomic implications. The statement that "ancient DNA has never been published from any open-air archaeological context in Java's volcanic interior" is correct but trivial. **No human skeletal remains of secure pre-400 CE date have been published from open-air contexts in Java's volcanic interior.** You cannot recover aDNA from material that has not been found. The "preservation differential" argument is a tautology: we haven't found bones, therefore aDNA isn't preserved; the lack of aDNA proves the bones are not preserved. Modern genomic data showing deep ancestry is consistent with *any* population history model involving long-term habitation and does not require a million-strong, state-level "civilization" in the 1st millennium CE. This channel is not independent evidence; it repackages the absence of physical human remains as a positive taphonomic signature.
    *   **Channel 5 (Colonial Archive):** This is data dredging. Applying NLP to colonial reports with a pre-defined goal of finding "volcano-distance gradients" and depth correlations is a recipe for finding patterns, whether meaningful or not. The "2.4% of finds within 0-15 km of volcanoes" statistic is meaningless without a null model that accounts for colonial survey bias (they focused on known monumental sites, which are often in fertile basins, not on active slopes). The match between reported find depths (1-4m) and the sedimentation model is intriguing but circumstantial; these finds are overwhelmingly from the Hindu-Buddhist period, not the pre-400 CE horizon. This channel mines noise, not signal.
    *   **Channel 6 (Archaeometry):** This is the strongest channel, but it is misapplied. The presence of *Jatim* beads and Pejeng-type drums in extra-Javanese contexts in the 4th-6th centuries CE is solid evidence for Javanese/Balinese production and participation in long-distance networks **during that period**. However, it does **not** prove the existence of a *pre-Hindu* civilization. The 4th-6th centuries CE are precisely the period of early Hindu-Buddhist state formation (Tarumanagara, etc.). This evidence is perfectly consistent with the conventional model of emerging, trade-oriented early states. The authors' attempt to use it as evidence for an *earlier*, *invisible* substrate is a chronological sleight of hand. The "invisibility-of-workshops" is an archaeological problem, but not one unique to a pre-Hindu era; early historic period workshops are also elusive.

3.  **The Argument is Fundamentally Unfalsifiable in its Current Form.** The paper pays lip service to falsification but constructs a defensive belt of auxiliary hypotheses. If no sites are found, it's because they are buried too deep. If deep coring finds nothing, the sample was inadequate. If linguistic analysis contradicts the substrate claim, the methodology was flawed. The core claim—a vast, invisible civilization—retreats behind a wall of compounded taphonomic filters that are invoked to explain the very absence they are meant to overcome. This is not a testable hypothesis; it is a just-so story.

4.  **Disregard for Existing Archaeological Research.** The manuscript dismisses over a century of archaeological work in Java with a wave of the hand, attributing the "empty" record to survey bias and "self-fulfilling" assumptions. This is both arrogant and incorrect. Intensive survey and excavation have been conducted in volcanic basins (e.g., the Progo Valley, the Malang Plain) by Indonesian and international teams. They have found abundant sites from the 8th-15th centuries CE, often just below the plow zone. The abrupt *termination* of this record before the 5th century is a real feature, not an artifact of method. The paper fails to engage seriously with the material culture sequences that *do* exist and how a hypothesized million-person civilization could have left no discernible impact on the ceramic, lithic, or metallurgical record that emerges later.

### 4. METHODOLOGICAL CONCERNS
*   **Statistical & Evidential Methods:** The "convergence" argument is qualitative and post-hoc. There is no statistical test for the independence or joint probability of the six channels. The channels are not independent: Channels 1, 2, 4, and 5 all share the same underlying assumption about population size (from Section 2). Channels 3 and 6 are the only ones that might be methodologically independent, but as argued, they do not support the specific claim.
*   **Multiple Comparisons & Confounders:** The Philippines comparison (Channel 2) is a classic case of ignoring confounders. Differences in geology, historical interaction spheres, subsistence bases (e.g., the importance of shell middens in the early Philippine economy vs. potential early rice in Java), and research history are all swept aside in favor of the single variable of volcanism.
*   **Falsification Criterion:** The proposed falsification tests are impractical or misdirected. "Systematic deep coring... across a statistically adequate sample" to 4-10m depth is a multi-million dollar, decades-long project that will never be funded to test a speculative hypothesis. The other falsification criteria (e.g., reanalysis of linguistic data) are about undermining individual channels, not the core thesis.
*   **Reproducibility:** The computational methods for the colonial archive mining and linguistic analysis are described only in reference to supplementary materials (E091, E141, E208). Their validity cannot be assessed from the manuscript alone. The heavy reliance on "E" numbered supplements, which are not provided for review, is a major barrier to evaluation.

### 5. SPECIFIC CLAIMS TO CHALLENGE
*   **Claim:** "Java at 400 CE supported roughly one to two million inhabitants." **Assessment: (d) Unfalsifiable/Overreach.** The estimate is derived from flawed models and is not grounded in any archaeological evidence from the period in question.
*   **Claim:** The contrast between Java and the Philippines is primarily explained by volcanic taphonomy. **Assessment: (c) Overreach.** It ignores fundamental historical and cultural differences and misuses comparative analogy.
*   **Claim:** Linguistic substrate evidence points to a pre-Hindu civilization in Java. **Assessment: (b) Plausible but under-supported.** It points to linguistic depth and contact, which is expected, but does not specify scale, material complexity, or location (Java vs. elsewhere).
*   **Claim:** The pattern of aDNA recovery is consistent with and predicted by the taphonomic framework. **Assessment: (c) Overreach.** It misrepresents the genetic data and constructs a tautology.
*   **Claim:** Colonial archive patterns "match the sedimentation calibration." **Assessment: (b) Plausible but under-supported.** The depth correlation is interesting but pertains to a later period and may reflect survey/recording bias.
*   **Claim:** Exported archaeometric materials (beads, drums) attest to an organized Javanese production system in the 4th-6th centuries CE that is invisible locally. **Assessment: (a) Well-supported for the period, (c) Overreach for the thesis.** The evidence is strong for production and trade *during the early historic period*, but it does not support the existence of a *pre-Hindu* ("invisible") civilization. It supports the emerging, visible early states.

### 6. WHAT THE PAPER DOES WELL
1.  **Provocative Question:** It forcefully raises the important, under-theorized issue of volcanic taphonomy in Island Southeast Asia and challenges archaeologists to consider the limits of surface visibility.
2.  **Interdisciplinary Ambition:** The attempt to synthesize geomorphology, linguistics, genomics, and historical documents is commendable in scope, even if the execution is flawed.
3.  **Highlighting Archaeometric Evidence:** The synthesis of the *Jatim* bead and Pejeng drum data is a valuable service, clearly outlining their chronological significance and distribution.

### 7. WHAT THE PAPER SHOULD BE, IF NOT THIS
A publishable paper would be far more modest and methodologically rigorous. It could be titled: "Volcanic Sedimentation and the Archaeological Visibility Horizon in Java: A Geoarchaeological Model and Its Implications." This paper would:
*   Focus exclusively on Channel 1: present the robust sedimentation rate calibration from the companion paper.
*   Clearly model the detection horizon and its implications for survey methodology.
*   **Drop the demographic speculation and the claim of a specific "invisible civilization."**
*   Use the Philippines comparison not as a "natural experiment" proving a hypothesis, but as a cautionary example of how different preservation regimes create different archaeological landscapes.
*   Briefly note that the early historic period material exports (beads/drums) present an interesting paradox (workshops are elusive) that future research, guided by the sedimentation model, might address.
*   Conclude with testable, practical recommendations for subsurface prospecting in volcanic Java, without pre-supposing what will be found.

### 8. ONE QUESTION FOR THE AUTHORS
What specific, *material* evidence from a securely dated, pre-4th century CE context in the volcanic interior of Java—even a single potsherd, lithic scatter, or posthole—would cause you to abandon the "compound taphonomic erasure" hypothesis and accept that the archaeological record, while incomplete, is broadly indicative of a low-density, non-urban population prior to the Hindu-Buddhist period?

**Summary for Editor:** This reviewer recommends rejection. The manuscript makes an extraordinary claim—a massive, archaeologically erased pre-Hindu civilization in Java—but supports it with a chain of weak, often circular arguments misrepresented as "convergent independent evidence." The demographic model is flawed, the comparative analysis is uncontrolled, and key channels (linguistics, genomics) are misapplied to support an archaeological claim they cannot address. The paper's core logic is unfalsifiable and displays a concerning disregard for the existing archaeological record. While the topic of volcanic taphonomy is important, this treatment is not scientifically rigorous. The authors should be advised to drastically scale back their claims, focus on their (potentially valid) geoarchaeological sedimentation model, and resubmit a paper that engages constructively with archaeological method and theory.
