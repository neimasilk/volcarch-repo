**Authors:** Mukhlis Amien & Go Frendi Gunawan

### 1. SUMMARY
This manuscript argues that the apparent absence of a pre-4th century CE archaeological record in the volcanic interior of Java is not evidence of a low population or late development, but of a systematic taphonomic bias. The authors propose that a substantial "invisible civilisation" existed, with a population on the order of 1-2 million by 400 CE, but its organic settlement matrix has been erased by cumulative volcanic sedimentation and tropical decay. This central claim is supported by the purported convergence of six independent lines of evidence: 1) volcanic sedimentation rates that bury pre-Hindu deposits beyond survey depth, 2) a stark contrast with the rich pre-400 CE record of the Philippines, 3) linguistic substrate analysis suggesting deep, pre-Indic vocabulary, 4) paleogenomic data indicating deep population continuity, 5) text mining of colonial archives revealing patterns consistent with burial, and 6) archaeometric evidence for Javanese/Balinese production (bronze drums, glass beads) found abroad but not in local workshops. The paper's central contribution is a methodological framework for diagnosing "selective survival" and a set of pre-registered predictions for testing it.

### 2. OVERALL ASSESSMENT
**Reject.** The core argument is an extraordinary claim built on a foundation of methodological overreach, conflated evidence channels, and a fundamental misreading of the archaeological process. The paper substitutes computational pattern-seeking for archaeological verification, constructing a grand narrative that is ultimately unfalsifiable in its current form.

### 3. MAJOR CONCERNS
1.  **The "Demographic Puzzle" is a Straw Man.** The entire argument hinges on a population estimate of 1-2 million for 400 CE Java, derived from ecological carrying capacity and back-projection. The methods in Section 2 are not robust. Carrying capacity estimates using ethnographic analogies (Bellwood 2017, etc.) are maximally optimistic and ignore the specific historical trajectory of agricultural intensification. Back-projection from 1600 CE is notoriously unreliable over such spans and assumes constant growth rates, which is indefensible. The paper dismisses the possibility of rapid post-400 CE growth as a "lower bound" but fails to engage with the substantial archaeological and historical consensus that the adoption of iron tools, wet-rice cultivation (sawah), and new political structures *did* catalyze significant demographic change. The "puzzle" is an artifact of the authors' own inflated baseline. If the pre-400 CE population was 150,000-300,000 (a plausible figure from a more conservative reading), the expected settlement count drops by an order of magnitude, dramatically altering the perceived gap.

2.  **Channels are Not Independent or Conclusive.** The paper's entire rhetorical weight rests on the "convergence" of six independent channels. This is illusory.
    *   **Channel 2 (Philippines) and Channel 1 (Sedimentation):** The comparison is invalid. The Philippines' archaeological richness is not just due to "lower volcanic density" but to decades of intensive, problem-oriented survey and excavation, often in advance of development, and a very different geological substrate with extensive karst. To claim the contrast is primarily due to taphonomy ignores the vast disparity in research intensity and geological context. Java's interior has not been surveyed with the same systematic, deep-testing methodology as many Philippine regions.
    *   **Channel 3 (Linguistics):** The findings are interesting but irrelevant to the core thesis. A deep Austronesian substrate and domain-specific borrowing from Sanskrit are well-established facts in historical linguistics. They demonstrate cultural and linguistic continuity, which no archaeologist disputes. They do **not** provide evidence for the scale, density, or "civilisational" complexity of a pre-4th century Javanese society. This channel speaks to *cultural* depth, not *archaeological* presence.
    *   **Channel 4 (Genomics):** This is a serious misrepresentation. The authors state the genomic data "requires a substantial, continuously inhabited local population well before the 4th century CE." This is true only at a trivial level: it requires a population large enough to not go extinct. Genetic continuity from the Neolithic does not mandate a population in the millions by 1 CE. The data is consistent with a small, stable population that later expanded. Furthermore, the discussion of ancient DNA preservation is circular, as they admit: the absence of aDNA is because we lack skeletal remains, which is the very absence the paper seeks to explain.
    *   **Channel 5 (Colonial Archives):** This is data dredging. Applying a VOLCARCH-informed NLP model to colonial texts and finding patterns that match VOLCARCH predictions is a textbook case of confirmation bias. Without a pre-registered, blinded analysis protocol, these "patterns" (volcano-distance gradient, depth distribution) are meaningless. The colonial archive is itself a product of biased discovery (focused on monuments, roads, and Dutch administrative centers).
    *   **Channel 6 (Archaeometry):** This is the strongest channel, but it is misapplied. Pejeng drums and *Jatim* beads demonstrate sophisticated craft production and participation in long-distance networks. This is valuable evidence for complex society. However, it does **not** prove the existence of a widespread, densely populated "civilisation" in Java's interior. It is perfectly consistent with coastal or peri-coastal entrepôts or cult centers. The "invisibility" of workshops is an interesting problem, but not evidence for their location being in the deep volcanic interior versus the now-submerged north coast or other non-volcanic zones.

3.  **The Argument is Fundamentally Unfalsifiable.** The paper claims falsifiability for each channel, but the overall thesis is armored against disproof. If deep coring finds nothing, it can be attributed to not coring in the right spot. If surveys find no sites, the burial depth was miscalculated or the population was more dispersed. The "selective survival" framework explains both the presence of certain artifacts (durable goods exported) and the absence of others (organic settlements), making it a non-refutable tautology. Good archaeological theory must take risks; this one explains everything and therefore nothing.

4.  **Misunderstanding of Archaeological Practice and Taphonomy.** The authors treat "the archaeological record" as a passive, uniformly sampled dataset. It is not. The absence of pre-400 CE sites in the Javanese interior is, in part, a function of research questions. Indonesian archaeology has historically focused on the monumental cores of known historical kingdoms. Systematic, full-coverage pedestrian survey designed to find small, buried settlements (like those common in the Philippines or the Near East) has been exceptionally rare in Java's volcanic basins. The paper mistakes an *under-explored* record for an *empty* one. Furthermore, their taphonomic model is overly simplistic. Volcanic sedimentation is not a uniform blanket; it creates a complex landscape of erosion, deposition, and re-exposure. Sites *can* and *do* survive within volcanic sequences, often in protected geomorphological settings.

### 4. METHODOLOGICAL CONCERNS
*   **Statistical Methods:** The demographic modeling in Section 2 lacks any measure of uncertainty or sensitivity analysis beyond simple scenario bounding. The use of Pearson's *r* for burial depth correlation (P1-core) is noted but its assumptions and the potential for outlier-driven results are not critically examined.
*   **Hidden Dependence:** The "independence" of channels is grossly overstated. Channels 1, 2, and 5 all share a dependency on the same underlying assumption of a large missing population. Channels 3 and 4 are interpreted through the lens of that same assumption. They are not epistemically independent lines converging on a truth; they are different observations being forced into the same interpretive mold.
*   **Confounders in Comparisons:** The Philippines comparison (Channel 2) fails to control for critical confounders: differential sea-level change and coastal progradation (hugely significant for island archaeology), the intensity and methodology of archaeological research over the last 50 years, and the role of karst not just as a preservative but as a focal point for human activity. The within-Java contrast (volcanic vs. non-volcanic) is more compelling but is mentioned only in passing and not developed as a controlled natural experiment.
*   **Falsification Criterion:** The proposed falsification tests are either impractical (systematic deep coring across Java) or easily dismissed (re-dating Pejeng drums would just mean "the pre-Hindu anchor shifts"). There is no single, clear finding that would cause the authors to abandon the core thesis.
*   **Reproducibility:** The computational methods for the colonial archive text mining are described only in reference to supplementary materials (E091, E141). The details of the NLP model, the training data, the named-entity recognition rules, and the geocoding protocol are absent from the manuscript, making this critical channel irreproducible.

### 5. SPECIFIC CLAIMS TO CHALLENGE
*   **Claim: "Java at 400 CE supported roughly one to two million inhabitants."** **Assessment: (c) Overreach.** The methodological basis for this figure is weak and ignores alternative, lower population models that are equally consistent with the vague comparative data.
*   **Claim: The six evidence channels "converge on the existence of such an erased substrate civilisation."** **Assessment: (d) Unfalsifiable / (c) Overreach.** The channels do not converge; they are stacked. The interpretation of each is stretched to support the predetermined conclusion. "Civilisation" is a loaded term implying urbanism, hierarchy, and monumentality, for which there is no evidence presented here beyond the capacity to produce bronze drums and glass beads.
*   **Claim: The pattern is "diagnosable computationally."** **Assessment: (c) Overreach.** This privileges computational pattern recognition over archaeological context. It is a potentially dangerous precedent, suggesting that models can replace excavation.
*   **Claim: The linguistic substrate evidence supports the existence of a pre-Hindu *civilisation*.** **Assessment: (b) Plausible but under-supported.** It supports a pre-Hindu *culture* and *language community*. There is a vast logical leap from vocabulary to the scale and complexity implied by "civilisation."

### 6. WHAT THE PAPER DOES WELL
1.  **Synthesizes Disparate Data:** The paper ambitiously brings together literature from volcanology, linguistics, genomics, archaeometry, and colonial history. This interdisciplinary scope is commendable and highlights questions that deserve more attention.
2.  **Asks a Provocative Question:** It forcefully challenges the tacit assumption that absence of evidence is evidence of absence in a geologically dynamic region. This is a healthy corrective, even if the proposed answer is flawed.
3.  **Highlights the Archaeometric Evidence (Channel 6):** The compilation of data on Pejeng drums and *Jatim* beads is valuable. It clearly shows that Java/Bali was a producer of high-status goods within long-distance networks earlier than sometimes acknowledged. This section, if divorced from the grand "invisible civilisation" narrative, could be the core of a strong paper on early craft production and exchange.

### 7. WHAT THE PAPER SHOULD BE, IF NOT THIS
A modest and publishable paper would abandon the demographic straw man and the convergence argument. It would focus on **Channel 1 (Sedimentation)** and **Channel 6 (Archaeometry)** as its core.
*   **Title:** "Taphonomic Challenges and Early Complex Society in Volcanic Java: Insights from Sedimentation Rates and Exported Material Culture."
*   **Argument:** The high volcanic sedimentation rates of Central and East Java have created a profound exploration bias, burying the archaeological record of the 1st millennium CE beyond the reach of standard survey. This bias must be actively corrected for in research design. One indicator of what we might be missing is the sophisticated production evidenced by exported goods (Pejeng drums, *Jatim* beads), whose local production contexts remain elusive. The paper would then present a focused, critical review of the sedimentation calibration data, a detailed analysis of the archaeometric evidence, and a discussion of methodological implications for survey (e.g., prioritizing geophysical survey in areas of predicted shallow burial, coring strategies). The linguistic and genomic data could be mentioned briefly as *context* for cultural continuity, not as *evidence* for a hidden population.

### 8. ONE QUESTION FOR THE AUTHORS
What specific, feasible archaeological finding—a result from a survey, excavation, or coring program—would definitively cause you to reject the hypothesis that a million-person pre-4th century civilisation lies buried in the Javanese interior, and to instead accept that the population was significantly smaller and its archaeological signature genuinely sparse?

---

**Summary for Editor:** This reviewer recommends rejection. The manuscript makes an extraordinary claim—a massive, archaeologically erased civilisation in pre-4th century Java—but fails to provide extraordinary evidence. Its central demographic estimate is poorly supported, its six "independent" lines of evidence are neither independent nor conclusive, and its overall argument is structured to be unfalsifiable. The paper confuses computational pattern-matching with archaeological proof and misunderstands the formation of the archaeological record. While it raises a valid point about taphonomic biases in volcanic regions and usefully synthesizes archaeometric data on early exports, these elements are buried within a speculative and methodologically unsound narrative. The paper would require a complete restructuring around a more modest and testable core premise to be suitable for this journal.
